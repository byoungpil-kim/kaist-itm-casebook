Innovation in Korea's Early-Morning Fresh Food Delivery Market: The Case of Oasis

Combine a management-of-technology theme with an industry to see cases that match both.

In a Korean early-morning fresh food delivery market governed by the maxim that "early-morning delivery means losses," Oasis — a mid-sized company that began as a logistics and distribution agent for an organic consumer co-operative — recorded a profit from its very first year online in 2019 and became the only profitable player in the market. This case study analyzes, through blue ocean strategy and the VRIO framework, the core capabilities and differentiation strategy that let Oasis deliver results without economies of scale, against competitors armed with technology and capital such as Kurly and SSG.com.

Research summary
  • Firms face many challenges in the Fourth Industrial Revolution but struggle to produce innovation results in response.
  • In Korea, innovation performance is particularly weak in certain service sectors such as transport and warehousing, and this study seeks to draw out lessons that firms in those sectors can benchmark.
  • As an innovation case it analyzes Oasis, a logistics and distribution company that entered the fresh food early-morning delivery market successfully through a differentiation strategy grounded in core capabilities and blue ocean strategy.
  • Oasis is the first case to break the received wisdom that early-morning delivery means losses, offering meaningful insight on innovation to other logistics and distribution firms.

1. Introduction

Background and purpose

According to the Korea Innovation Survey by the Science and Technology Policy Institute (STEPI), only 24% of manufacturing firms and 17% of service firms achieved results qualifying as innovation over the three years 2020–2022. Looking within services, information and communications and arts and sports showed innovation rates of 10–16%, whereas transport and warehousing, health and welfare, and finance and insurance came in below 2% — an imbalance in innovation performance across sectors. This study selects Oasis as an innovation case worth attention in transport and warehousing, one of the lowest-performing sectors.

Oasis began in 2011 as Woori Networks, a logistics and distribution agent for the organic consumer co-operative Woori Co-op, expanded into consignment sales at the co-operative's offline stores in 2017, and launched its own e-commerce platform Oasis Market in 2019. Within two years of entering the fresh food early-morning delivery market it ranked among the top three by share — a result achieved in competition with Kurly, which leads with AI demand forecasting, and SSG.com, which built advanced logistics centers with large-corporate capital. Above all, it recorded KRW 1 billion in operating profit in 2019, its first year online, breaking the formula that early-morning delivery means losses, and has never recorded a loss even as revenue grew from KRW 142.3 billion in 2019 to KRW 427.4 billion in 2022.

Defining the fresh food early-morning delivery market

Korea's e-commerce market grew 53%, from KRW 134 trillion in transaction value in 2019 to about KRW 206 trillion in 2022, as COVID-19 expanded contactless purchasing, and the online food market grew from KRW 10 trillion in 2017 to KRW 31 trillion in 2021. The inflection point came in 2015 when Kurly (then The Farmers) launched the industry's first "Saetbyeol Delivery," and the early-morning delivery market has since grown more than tenfold, from KRW 0.8 trillion in 2019 to a projected KRW 8.5 trillion in 2022.

Among the market's main players, Kurly recorded KRW 2 trillion in revenue and an operating loss of KRW 228.9 billion in 2022 (an operating margin of −11%), while SSG.com recorded KRW 1.6 trillion in revenue and an operating loss of KRW 110.2 billion. Many companies — Lotte Shopping (Lotte On), BGF and GS Retail among them — withdrew from the market as losses persisted under large capital investment in cold chain infrastructure and night-shift labor costs roughly 1.5 times daytime rates. In this market Oasis alone has stayed profitable.

2. Theoretical background

The study uses four theoretical frames. First, Kim and Mauborgne's value innovation and blue ocean strategy, which refuses to treat cost and customer value as a trade-off and pursues differentiation and low cost simultaneously, using the four actions framework — eliminate, reduce, raise, create (ERRC) — and the strategy canvas. Second, Prahalad and Hamel's core competency and Barney's VRIO framework (value, rarity, inimitability, organization) to test how a firm's resources contribute to competitive advantage. Third, the late mover advantage proposed by Shankar and others: a latecomer can ride on the market a pioneer has opened, saving marketing cost and reducing risk through the pioneer's trial and error. Fourth, theories of switching cost and customer lock-in.

3. Innovation case study

Oasis Market's core capabilities

① Experience and expertise in fresh food distribution and logistics. Management and merchandisers who came from the co-operative have grown alongside organic producers in agriculture, fisheries and livestock in the field. Of some 800 suppliers, 70–80% have traded with the company for at least ten years, since the launch of Woori Co-op in 2009 or earlier, and do not switch easily even when competitors court them with higher prices. This is the result of twelve years of growing together with payment terms arranged in the producers' favor and with little friction. Sourcing is exacting too: the company handles only products certified antibiotic-free or pesticide-free or better, allows no food additives such as MSG or color fixatives, and treats preserving its identity as a seller of Korean eco-friendly organic produce as a long-term aim rather than expanding its range.

② Omnichannel capability specialized in organic produce. Because it expanded online from a base of operating consignment stores for the co-operative, Oasis can offer the advantages of offline and online at once. This is a major point of difference from online-only competitors: Kurly has no offline stores, and SSG.com, as a legal entity separate from E-mart, has none either.

③ Active software support from its IT parent. Its parent, Giosoft, is an IT services company founded in 1998 with expertise in software development specialized in handheld devices. This unusual structure — receiving active IT support from a software company that has internalized 25 years of technical capability under the same founder — is hard for competitors to imitate. All three capabilities were assessed as meeting both Prahalad and Hamel's criteria and the VRIO framework for core capabilities.

Oasis's late mover advantage and blue ocean strategy

When Oasis Market launched in 2019, Oasis and SSG.com, both latecomers, free-rode into the fresh food early-morning delivery market that Kurly had opened, without having to develop the market themselves. The direction of their innovation, however, was diametrically opposed. SSG.com used its large capital base to pursue full infrastructure automation (running into limits — it ended early-morning delivery in the Chungcheong region in December 2022 because of high maintenance costs), while Oasis chose value innovation, maximizing customer value while minimizing cost. In ERRC terms it eliminated unnecessary marketing and advertising cost, reduced early-morning delivery logistics cost through process innovation, raised the price competitiveness of core organic foods, and created a new customer experience through its omnichannel model.

ERRC ①: a differentiated logistics infrastructure strategy

Like Coupang, Kurly and SSG.com adopted large customer fulfillment centers (CFCs) entailing high up-front cost, aiming to turn profitable through economies of scale. Oasis chose selection and focus instead of scale, reinterpreting and designing the small "dark store" model with minimal automation as a logistics center dedicated to early-morning delivery. Where competitors' centers pick first by ambient, chilled and frozen zone and then regroup items by customer, Oasis does not divide its storage space into zones: a picker fills a single basket and everything is consolidated into one box for dispatch, dramatically cutting walking distance and time.

Labor efficiency is likewise maximized. A dark store picking cart normally tops out at around six baskets for fear of mix-ups, but Oasis's own cart carries up to 20. What makes this possible is OASiS Route, handheld software developed in-house. When a worker scans the QR code on an order confirmation, the software guides the optimal picking route like a navigation system, eliminating human error. It runs on an ordinary mobile phone with no specialized device, and functions can be developed as needed at low cost through the parent company. As a result, tangible assets as a share of revenue are just 6%, against competitors' 17–39.8%, and despite the labor-intensive dark store model, personnel costs as a share of revenue are comparable to or lower than those of CFC-based competitors (6.6–12.1%).

ERRC ②: inventory management built on maximizing sales

The central dilemma of early-morning delivery is the clash between avoiding stock-outs and minimizing waste. Competitors target 1% waste through AI demand forecasting, but this requires conservative forecasts 5–10% below expected sales, so revenue opportunities are lost to stock-outs. Oasis resolved the dilemma through integrated online–offline inventory management: online orders are fulfilled first and all remaining stock is supplied to offline stores for discounted sale. Achieving zero online waste allowed it to hold stock aggressively. Discount selling puts its valuation allowance at around 12%, up to 11.7 percentage points higher than competitors', but thanks to low procurement costs from its network of producers its final cost of sales ratio is the same as Kurly's and in fact lower than SSG.com's.

ERRC ③: winning loyal customers through product differentiation

In a fresh food market where the mix of product categories alone offers little differentiation, Oasis raised the quality and price competitiveness of core basics — milk, eggs, vegetables — to the highest level. Eggs in particular are sold at up to 40% below competitors' prices for the top grade, shell code 1. Social media keyword analysis shows heavy search around basic fresh ingredients for Oasis, indicating that its identity as a specializt seller of fresh basics has registered with customers. Monthly transactions rose from 160,000 in September 2020 to 634,000 in August 2023, and average transaction value rose 5%, from KRW 39,000 in March 2023 to KRW 41,000 in August.

ERRC ④: minimizing marketing and advertising spend

Unlike competitors that lean on celebrity marketing, Oasis uses its own offline stores as "billboards that generate revenue" — an inversion in which existing sales activity itself produces the marketing effect at no additional cost. Advertising and promotion spending is just 0.3% of revenue against 3–4% at SSG.com and Kurly, yet customers show a repeat purchase rate above 90% on an annual average, sustaining high loyalty.

The inversion worked: with no additional marketing spend, ordinary trading activity itself produced the marketing effect.

4. Conclusion and limitations

On the strength of three core capabilities — expertise in fresh food distribution and logistics, in-house software development, and omnichannel capability specialized in organic produce — Oasis exploited its late mover advantage to pursue four ERRC strategies, and achieved the innovation result of becoming the only profitable company in the early-morning delivery market without economies of scale.

The study has several limitations. Oasis is still small in revenue relative to competitors and its delivery area is limited, so whether it can sustain these results as the business expands remains an open question. In July 2022 it built a 100,000-square-meter logistics center in Uiwang, Gyeonggi Province, to widen its delivery area, and now operates it borrowing heavily from the methods used at its smaller centers. It remained profitable through the third quarter of 2023, but difficulties are inevitable as scale grows, and whether profitability holds needs continued attention. The study is also a qualitative case study confined to the fresh food early-morning delivery market, and quantitative research on levels of innovation and barriers to innovation by sector is proposed as follow-up work.

Implications
  • Identifying core capabilities must come first. A firm should examine Prahalad and Hamel's three requirements (customer value, differentiation, extendability) together with the four requirements of the VRIO framework to identify its own distinctive core capabilities clearly, and only then look for a differentiation strategy built on them.
  • Innovation is not the preserve of the market leader. Even a firm with low market share or a latecomer can innovate successfully if the direction of its strategy is right.
  • Blind investment in technology and infrastructure is not the best approach. As the early-morning delivery market shows, indiscriminate investment leads to continuing losses that undermine the sustainability of the business and can end in market withdrawal. What deserves attention is less the business model itself than the way Oasis pursued innovation — recognizing its core capabilities accurately and choosing a path of differentiation that competitors were not following.
  • Innovation results become the foundation for new businesses. Oasis is attempting various extensions, including commercializing OASiS Route, automating offline stores without staff, and "on-air delivery" and quick commerce through joint ventures.
