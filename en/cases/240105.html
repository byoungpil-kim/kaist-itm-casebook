From Technological Catch-up to Disruptive Innovation: A Case Study on the Success of T-50 Development

Aerospace is the most complex field of all industries and the hardest to integrate as a system: a single fighter aircraft contains some 200,000 parts. It has been seen as a field in which a latecomer struggles to succeed either in catching up technologically or in the market — yet Korea Aerospace Industries (KAI) succeeded in developing the world's eighth supersonic aircraft on the strength of licensed production experience alone. This case study analyzes the T-50 to identify the factors behind KAI's success in both technology development and the market.

Research summary
  • Aerospace is a national strategic technology directly tied to self-reliant defense and economic development, and a complex product system industry — the most complex of all and the hardest to integrate. Because accumulated experience matters so much, it is seen as a field in which a latecomer struggles to achieve either technological catch-up or market success.
  • Even so, Korea Aerospace Industries (KAI) succeeded in both developing supersonic aircraft technology and entering the market, and this study analyzes the T-50 case to identify the factors behind that success.
  • The factors behind successful technology development were converting tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge and an integrated organizational structure.
  • The factors behind market success were market-oriented development and targeting a beachhead market, and disruptive innovation.
  • The case suggests that for a latecomer entering aerospace, government policy and a firm's technological capability matter — but so, greatly, does the firm's management strategy: knowledge management, HR and organizational strategy, and market and marketing strategy.

1. Introduction

The more complex the technology and the higher the level of system integration an industry requires, the harder it is for a latecomer to enter and catch up. A car is made of 30,000 parts; a fighter aircraft, of about 200,000. Incumbents keep their knowledge tacit and hold it as heritage knowledge, where accumulated experience matters, in order to minimize the risk of being caught.

Even so, Korea became the only country to succeed at every stage in the short span of 30 years — from licensed production of the KF-16 through development of the T-50 advanced trainer to the development and export of the Surion (KUH) helicopter. The T-50 in particular, which KAI developed as the world's eighth such aircraft, is a case without parallel anywhere: rather than following the usual aircraft development sequence of licensed production, basic trainer, advanced trainer and then supersonic aircraft, KAI went straight from licensed production to a successful supersonic aircraft.

2. Theoretical background

2.1 The nature of aerospace — the most complex of complex product systems

Aerospace is a national strategic industry in terms of both security and industrial development, a technology-intensive industry converging advanced materials, electronics, mechanical engineering, communications and IT, and a high-value-added industry — it is said that exporting one fighter aircraft has the effect of exporting 1,000 domestically built mid-size cars. It is also a long-cycle industry: where development takes an average of 1.5 years in automotive and five years in shipbuilding, aerospace averages ten.

2.2 National strategic technology and technological sovereignty

Amid US–China technological rivalry, and with unmanned aircraft spreading across the battlefield and urban air mobility (UAM) approaching commercialization, aerospace has been designated one of Korea's twelve national strategic technologies. A national strategic technology is defined as one essential at national level to security or economic prosperity.

2.4 Tacit and explicit knowledge

In aerospace, knowledge of individual components can be generalized into documents, but the knowledge of integrating them into a single system is abstract, and experience accumulated over time plays a critical role. Aerospace is therefore an industry of tacit knowledge, where heritage knowledge matters. The study uses Nonaka's (1994) SECI model — socialization, externalization, combination, internalization — as its framework.

2.5 Disruptive innovation

Disruptive innovation is "innovation that disrupts the existing market order and creates a new market by supplying simple, convenient and inexpensive products to less demanding customers" (Christensen, 2013). Because incumbents cannot change their resources, processes and values (RPV) quickly, they struggle to prepare even when they can see disruption coming.

3. Research questions

Aerospace is a national strategic technology directly tied to self-reliant defense and economic development, and at the same time a complex product system (CoPS) industry so hard to integrate that a single fighter comprises some 200,000 parts. That complexity gives its knowledge a tacit character, so accumulated past experience is decisive. From this the study sets three research questions. First, what factors allowed Korea Aerospace Industries (KAI), a latecomer in fighter development, to succeed in technological catch-up? Second, what factors allowed KAI as a latecomer to succeed in the market? Third, what does this suggest for latecomers seeking to build an aerospace industry?

To answer them, the study analyzes the T-50 (FA-50) case in two periods — the technology development period and the market entry period after development was complete and sales began — reviews the technological and business results of successful development and market entry, and then draws the factors behind the successful catch-up together.

4. Case analysis: fifteen years of T-50 development

The T-50 program was Korea's first supersonic aircraft development project, begun to replace the air force's ageing trainers (T-33 and TF-5B). It was a large national project running from October 1997 to September 2005 with a budget of KRW 1,799.6 billion, conducted as international joint development with the government bearing 70%, KAI 17% and America's Lockheed Martin 13%.

4.2.2 The technology development period — from offset trade to full-scale development

At the preliminary research stage Korea had no technology to develop a supersonic aircraft, so development technology was transferred through offset trade on the advanced trainer the air force was purchasing (from Britain's BAE). At the exploratory development stage the requirement was set as "developing the world's first supersonic advanced trainer," and it was decided that full-scale development would be led directly by a company rather than a national research institute — on the judgement that a company, with its survival at stake, would be more likely to succeed.

At the full-scale development stage all design was done 100% with computer-aided design software (CATIA), an unusual approach hard to find anywhere in the world at the time. Repeated simulation allowed assembly with no error whatsoever, and the first prototype made its maiden flight in August 2002 and first broke the sound barrier in February 2003.

The T-50 is a case without parallel in the history of world aerospace: a successful supersonic aircraft developed straight from licensed production.

4.2.3 The market entry period — one platform, many variants

After successful development, the basic T-50 platform was adapted into a lead-in fighter trainer (TA-50), an aerobatic aircraft (T-50B) and a light attack aircraft (FA-50). Tailored to export customers' requirements it went to Indonesia (T-50i) and the Philippines (FA-50PH), and most recently to a large FA-50PL export contract with Poland worth KRW 3.4 trillion.

4.3 Technological and business results

Through T-50 development KAI secured technological capability close to that of advanced countries (approaching a technology level of 5.0) in systems integration, aircraft assembly, design and analysis, and test and evaluation, and Korea joined the twelve countries worldwide possessing a domestically developed supersonic aircraft. Patent filings surged to 380 during full-scale development. Revenue has risen continuously since 2006, when the T-50 was successfully developed and entered the market, and over the past five years the T-50 family has settled in as a stable cash cow accounting for an average of 30% of revenue.

5. Findings: four success factors

5.1 Converting tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge

KAI took in accumulated experience as tacit knowledge through one-to-one technical instruction with experienced technical advisers (T/As) seconded from Lockheed Martin (socialization), and made that tacit knowledge explicit by documenting not only official drawings and technical data but also knowledge acquired informally (externalization). Storing the documented explicit knowledge in an internal information-sharing system created more systematic explicit knowledge (combination), and engineers formed study groups at research team level to discuss it, internalizing the knowledge again. The knowledge accumulated this way was actively used in the later development of the Surion (KUH-1), the Light Armed Helicopter (LAH) and the KF-21 fighter.

5.2 Integrated organizational structure

An aircraft is a product of integral architecture: even a minor design change to the engine affects every system — fuel, power transmission, airframe structure, electrical wiring, even the cockpit instrument panel. To control that complexity, KAI placed program management, engineering, marketing, procurement, production quality and management support all within a single integrated organization, the KTX-2 (T-50) Development Center, so that development issues could be met with organic communication and a company-wide response.

5.3 Market-oriented development and targeting a beachhead market

KAI built a market-oriented development plan from the planning stage. Instead of the fighter market, where advanced firms compete fiercely, it targeted the less contested market for an advanced trainer that doubles as a light attack aircraft, taking a complementary position that drew Lockheed Martin's investment (13%), active technical support and global marketing capability. Anticipating a market of about 1,000 aircraft including 400 for the US Air Force's next-generation advanced trainer, it built the design concept from the outset so the aircraft could be adapted into a light attack variant — and actual sales have developed much as that forecast anticipated.

5.4 Disruptive innovation

Incumbents such as Boeing and Lockheed Martin overlooked the low-margin market for an advanced trainer doubling as a light attack aircraft, pursuing a sustaining strategy of satisfying the most demanding customers with the latest fighters. KAI, by contrast, produced a low-end disruption by supplying the T-50 (FA-50) — a lower-performance, lower-cost, appropriately specified product — to less demanding customers.

By offering countries that would value even a single additional fighter a multi-role aircraft serving as both advanced trainer and light attack aircraft, KAI created a market — turning non-consumers into consumers.

At the time no aircraft existed that was supersonic while serving as both advanced trainer and light attack aircraft. Just as the software complexity of next-generation fighters such as the F-22 and F-35 was rising sharply and existing subsonic trainers (the M-346 and Hawk-128) were becoming inadequate for training, the Mach 1.5 T-50 dramatically shortened pilot conversion training — achieving new-market disruption as well.

Implications
  • Why the technological catch-up succeeded: A documentation capability that made every R&D plan, piece of technical knowledge and functional responsibility explicit from the outset (converting tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge), together with an integrated organizational structure controlling the integral architecture's complexity, drove successful supersonic aircraft development.
  • Why the market entry succeeded: A market-oriented design concept from the R&D planning stage, complementary product positioning that drew investment from an advanced firm, and disruptive innovation supplying an appropriately specified product to a market advanced firms had overlooked all proved effective.
  • What this suggests for latecomers: In a highly public industry such as fighter aircraft, requiring KRW 5–10 trillion of investment and more than ten years of development, active government technology demand policy creating domestic R&D opportunities is essential. But even with government policy behind it, success is unlikely unless the firm's capability and strategy to absorb it are ready — knowledge management, HR and organizational strategy, and market and marketing strategy must all work together.
