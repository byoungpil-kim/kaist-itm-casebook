DJI's Innovation Strategy through Patent Analysis

As technological progress and shifting social demand drive rapid growth in the drone market, China's DJI (大疆创新) leads the civilian drone industry with a 70% share of the global market. It is an unusual case of a Chinese firm — long seen as a manufacturing latecomer — pre-empting a frontier technology market. This study collects 1,163 patents filed by DJI in the United States, China, Japan, Korea and Europe, empirically analyzes the direction of its technology development and the flow of technological knowledge, and identifies the substance of DJI's innovation strategy.

Research summary
  • As the drone market grows rapidly and its range of applications widens, the civilian drone industry is led by China's DJI, which holds 70% of the global market. It is an unusual case of a Chinese firm pre-empting a frontier technology market. Using DJI's patent data, this study analyzes the direction of its technology development and the flow of technological knowledge to identify its innovation strategy.
  • DJI is strengthening R&D in high-value-added fields where drone technology can be extended and applied.
  • Drone technology has the character of an applied and incremental technology that advances by building on prior art, and prior art likewise played a large role in DJI's technological innovation. DJI cited prior art from US and Japanese firms extensively, while in some areas such as batteries its own development results are evident.
  • DJI is expected to move toward a solutions business (DaaS) providing drone-related services.
  • The core of DJI's innovation capability is its technology absorption capability. It appears to have grown out of the stage of absorbing technology into a stage of combining the technologies it needs and leading innovation itself.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background — an unmanned aviation industry poised for dynamic growth

Unmanned aircraft were once centered on military drones, but the market is expected to grow substantially around civilian commercial drones used for hobbies, broadcast filming, delivery and the like. According to Teal Group, a US market analysis firm, the global unmanned aircraft market is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of more than 10% between 2019 and 2024. Unmanned aviation has long development cycles and high barriers to entry in capital and technology, but it is a knowledge-based industry in which successful entrants can generate stable returns over a long period.

1.2 Why DJI

Unlike other Fourth Industrial Revolution technology fields led by global US firms, the civilian commercial drone industry is led by China's DJI. It is hard to find another field in which a firm outside the United States holds the leading technology and dominates the world market. Nor does it resemble cases such as Huawei or Tencent, which caught up in industries that had grown in advanced economies. Civilian commercial drones are an early-stage industry that only recently began to grow, and DJI has behaved not as an imitator or a chaser but as a market leader creating its own path of innovation.

DJI pioneered the civilian drone market and drove its growth, taking the top position in the drone industry and changing perceptions of "Made in China."

1.3 Why patent analysis

A patent is an exclusive right granted to an invention and a representative indicator of technological innovation (Ernst, 2003). If the number of patent applications reflects an innovator's stock of intellectual property and its innovation performance, the citation relationships among patents reflect the flow of intellectual property and the potential for creating new knowledge. This study uses network analysis to examine the structural features of that knowledge flow. By visualizing nodes and links, it shows how many technologies are connected and how actively knowledge flows among them.

2. Research subject: DJI's growth and market creation

2.1 The growth of DJI

DJI (SZ DJI Technology Co., Ltd.) is a drone research, development and production company founded in Shenzhen, China, in 2006 by Frank Wang. It took only three years to move from making the flight controller (FC), a core drone component, to producing complete products equipped with artificial intelligence and onboard information processing. Some 2,600 people — 33% of all employees — work as dedicated research staff, and the company invests 7% of revenue in R&D each year, holding to a principle that secures technological capability above all else. "Even as the company grows, we keep research staff at a third of the workforce" is the CEO's philosophy. Revenue grew at a formidable pace, from CNY 3 million in 2010 to CNY 800 million in 2013, past USD 1 billion in 2015 and to USD 2.83 billion in 2017. Goldman Sachs data from 2014 put DJI at 70% of the global commercial drone market, far ahead of second-placed Parrot, and according to drone market research firm DRONII, DJI's share of the US drone market reached 76.8% in 2019.

2.2 Product development and market creation

In the early days there was no concept of a finished drone product; the field was dominated by DIY assembly, in which individuals put together components and downloaded software code themselves. DJI caused a stir by launching the Phantom, an all-in-one finished drone that could fly straight out of the box and came fitted with sensors, a control transmitter and receiver, a camera and a video transmitter and receiver. This expanded a narrow drone market previously confined to military use into consumer, industrial and public applications, and broadened the customer base from experts to non-experts. More recently, by releasing an SDK development platform along with GS Pro, Flight Hub and DJI Terra, the company has been growing into a total solutions provider that extends the drone platform ecosystem.

3. Patent analysis results

3.2 General patent analysis — the inflection point of 2014

Filings by year show a sharp increase from 2014, immediately after DJI's first all-in-one drone launched in 2013 (43 filings in 2013 rising to 328 in 2014). By country the split is China 39%, the United States 29%, Japan 26% and Europe 5%. DJI shifted its strategy from filing mainly at home in China toward filing through the PCT and then re-filing in the United States, strengthening legal protection in the actual drone market. In 2017 overseas sales accounted for 80% of DJI's total revenue. By main IPC, the patents are concentrated in flight (B64), communications (H04), control and regulation (G05), photography (G03) and machine elements (F16).

3.3 Analysis by technology — the center of gravity moves to mission equipment

Classifying the patent portfolio into seven technology categories — power, airframe (platform), flight control, electronic communications, mission equipment, ground support equipment and services — mission equipment accounted for the largest share at about 38.3%, followed by flight control (23.9%), airframe/platform (23.4%), power (7.5%) and electronic communications (4.8%). Until 2013, filings on airframe-related hardware predominated, but from 2014 onward mission equipment technologies — sensors, cameras, gimbals, spraying devices, data storage — have driven the overall filing count. From 2015, purely software technologies such as image processing and data storage were also filed in numbers, and service patents such as virtual sightseeing using drones stand out. The subject matter of DJI's patents is expanding into comprehensive technological content no longer confined to drone manufacturing.

3.4.1 Quantitative citation analysis — applied technology and the absorption of external knowledge

DJI made about 20,000 citations while receiving about 5,000, roughly four times as many citations made as received. This indicates that DJI's technology has the character of applied and incremental development built on prior art rather than of original core technology, because small drones build on the navigation, control, hardware design and manufacturing technologies of aircraft and military unmanned aircraft.

Classifying the applicants of the cited patents, corporate patents accounted for the largest share at 72.3%, followed by individuals at 17.4%, universities at 7.5% and public research institutes at 2.8%. More than a third of the cited corporate patents were held by US firms: some 1,100 DJI patents cited more than 4,900 US corporate patents. That is an average of four US technologies cited per patent. Citations to the United States were concentrated in aerospace firms such as Honeywell and Boeing and in global communications companies, while citations to Japan were concentrated in camera and image-processing firms such as Canon. Meanwhile, more than 40% of citations to Chinese firms were DJI's own self-citations, and these increased rapidly year by year — suggesting that the company absorbs technological knowledge from advanced economies, secures core technologies, and then strengthens its rights through self-citation.

3.4.2 Citation network visualization — where the technological knowledge came from

A network map linking the IPC classes of DJI's patents (upper nodes) to the applicants of the cited patents (lower nodes) shows that the sources of knowledge differ sharply by technology field. In performing operations and transporting (B), patents in aircraft, aviation and aerospace engineering are overwhelmingly numerous and the link to US firms is the thickest. This means that in aerospace engineering — the most central technology for drones — the United States holds the original technology and acts as the early disseminator of knowledge.

In physics (G), control and regulation and measurement technologies had the highest frequencies, and here too the link to US firms was thickest. By contrast, photography and optics showed their strongest connection to Japanese firms. This field is directly tied to aerial photography, and shows that DJI was strongly influenced by Japanese firms in imaging technology.

In electricity (H), telecommunications technologies related to video transmission and image data were strongly connected to Japanese and US firms, whereas "generation of electric power" — that is, drone battery technology — drew the most citations from Chinese firms (Chinese firms plus DJI itself). This indicates that China holds strong capability and competitiveness in the lithium polymer (LiPo) battery technology drones use.

In human necessities, fixed constructions and mechanical engineering (A, E, F), machine element technologies — including drone stabilization mounts and camera fixing devices — appeared frequently, and citations to Chinese firms and to DJI's own technology were the strongest. A high level of self-citation means the patent is an important foundational technology for the firm. DJI is indeed regarded as the world leader in camera stabilization devices such as Osmo and Ronin, and the patent analysis confirms that camera stabilization is a strategic technology that differentiates DJI from its competitors. Agriculture nodes also appear, though at low frequency, consistent with DJI's 2015 launch of the Agras agricultural drone and its push into crop spraying, seeding and other agricultural drone applications.

4. Conclusion

Taken together, the patent analysis classifies DJI's patents into five technologies — power, airframe (platform), flight control, electronic communications and mission equipment — with the share of mission equipment technology growing steadily since 2014. The citation analysis shows that DJI cited prior art from US firms most heavily in aircraft, aerospace engineering and control technologies and from Japanese firms in imaging and image processing, while in battery technology and camera fixing devices the development results of Chinese firms and of DJI itself are evident.

DJI's capacity to digest and absorb external knowledge worked in synergy with its own R&D capability to expand its technology clusters, and it is reasonable to infer that the company made dramatic progress in the process.
Implications
  • The move to DaaS: Given the surge in software-centered mission equipment patents and the emergence of service patents such as virtual sightseeing, DJI is expected to move beyond hardware manufacturing and expand into a solutions business providing drone-related services (DaaS: Drone as a Service) and into platform operation.
  • Absorptive capability is the core of innovation capability: In accumulating core drone technologies, DJI actively took in knowledge by citing a wide range of external prior art. Adopting, absorbing and exploiting external core technology necessarily required building outstanding R&D capability internally, and this technology absorption capability was the driving force behind DJI's technological capability.
  • From absorbing to leading: DJI has grown out of the stage of absorbing technology into a stage of combining the technologies it needs and leading innovation itself. In battery technology and some machine elements, the localization of Chinese technology and the accumulation of DJI's own core technology are evident — a Chinese firm that had drawn little attention from an innovation perspective is now developing core technology and propagating global standards.
