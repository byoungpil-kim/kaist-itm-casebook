Innovation in the Webtoon Business: Platform Business and the Flywheel Effect

Naver and Kakao entered a comics industry in decline and created an entirely new business: the webtoon. This study analyzes, through platform business theory and the flywheel effect, how a webtoon business that did not even exist until the early 2000s grew into a flagship revenue stream for both companies, and considers the global prospects of Korea's webtoon business.

Research summary
  • Naver and Kakao entered a comics industry in decline and created a new business in the webtoon. At the core of this innovation lie the platform business model, the amateur "Challenge Comics" board, paid monetization (a partial-payment model) and expansion into IP business.
  • The foundation of that platform business success is the flywheel effect. Built on the characteriztics of a multi-sided market, cross-side network effects and the chicken-and-egg problem, the webtoon platform turns a flywheel: more varied, higher-quality works → more readers → more platform revenue → more creator income → more creators → more varied, higher-quality works.
  • Webtoon companies that grew out of portals focused on continuously securing creators and works, and the innovations in the webtoon business acted as the force turning the flywheel by raising creator income. The Challenge Comics system secured many creators and works in a short time, and the fixed-fee system provided steady income independent of popularity, stabilizing creators' household finances.
  • Revenue models such as partial monetization and webtoon advertising created a structure in which creators could earn hundreds of thousands of dollars a year on performance. Through IP business, secondary works brought creators additional income, and the success of those works in turn raised webtoon viewership, generating revenue once again.
  • The flywheel is verified as working through actual data on the number of creators, works, readers and platform revenue.

1. Background and purpose

The webtoon is a genre of digital comics originating in Korea, and Korean webtoons already rank first among smartphone comics apps in more than 100 countries. Webtoon creators serializing on major platforms such as Naver and Kakao earn an average of KRW 310 million a year — not far short of the KRW 360 million average for non-registered executives at large corporations — and dramas and films based on webtoons, such as Sweet Home and Space Sweepers, have topped Netflix content rankings. Time spent on webtoons on mobile is comparable to OTT services, and in usage frequency and weekly user retention webtoons have already overtaken them. Their presence in paid spending is even more striking: in the September 2021 iOS app revenue rankings in Korea, webtoon-related apps held the largest share at 40% once games are excluded.

2. Theoretical background

2.1 Platform business

A platform business creates value by connecting different types of participants (producers, consumers and others) who previously found it hard to transact, and by facilitating their interaction. Most relevant here are the multi-sided market, cross-side network effects, and the chicken-and-egg problem of deciding which of two interdependent participant groups to attract first.

2.2 The flywheel effect

A flywheel is an automotive component that keeps rotating on inertia without power: it takes great force to set in motion, but once momentum builds it turns by itself. Jim Collins brought the principle into management in his 2001 book Good to Great, and Amazon's Jeff Bezos drew on it to make a virtuous cycle — lower prices → more customers → more sellers → wider selection → economies of scale → lower prices — the core strategy of Amazon's business model.

3. Understanding webtoons and the webtoon industry

3.1 Characteriztics and innovations of the webtoon business

Where earlier digital comics simply followed the format and framework of print comics, webtoons created a distinctive format and method of creation optimized for the smartphone: a dedicated page size, vertical scrolling and continuous panels. In business terms, the great innovation of the webtoon is bringing the platform business model into the comics market. The specific innovation points are: ① securing large volumes of quality webtoons quickly through the Challenge Comics system; ② succeeding at paid monetization by developing a partial-payment model; ③ actively developing IP business by linking web novels, webtoons, films and dramas; and ④ rapidly transplanting a business model proven at home into global markets.

The best known of the partial-payment models is Kakao's "Wait Until Free", inspired by the heart system in the mobile game Anipang. One episode becomes free after a set interval, but readers who do not want to wait can pay to read the next episode immediately. By leaving a free path open, it settled into the market without much resistance. The model also shaped the genre itself: a typical webtoon episode now runs to about 70 panels, so the content of one print comic volume is split across more than ten episodes, each priced low at KRW 200–300.

3.2 Market size and major players

Korea's comics market was worth KRW 954 billion in 2019 and has grown steadily at a CAGR of 4.5% since 2007. Print comics have stagnated while digital comics (42.2% of the market) have grown rapidly at a CAGR of 18.4%, driving the market as a whole. Even in Japan, the world's foremost comics power, a market that had been declining since the mid-1990s rebounded from 2014 on the rapid growth of digital comics (CAGR 25.2%). In Korea's webtoon market, measured by annual page views, Naver Webtoon holds a commanding first place at 65.1% and Kakao (Kakao Page plus Kakao Webtoon) second at 19.5%, so the two portal-born platforms account for 84.6% of all page views. In 2020 Naver Webtoon's worldwide transaction value was KRW 820 billion (revenue KRW 360.9 billion) and Kakao Webtoon's was KRW 769.4 billion (revenue KRW 528 billion).

4. The growth strategy of webtoon platforms: the flywheel effect

4.1 The problem facing portal-born platforms

On a webtoon platform the chicken and the egg are quality webtoons and the many readers who consume them. Naver and Kakao, already dominant in portal services, could draw readers relatively easily simply by placing a webtoon section on the portal's front page. But readers leave easily when they are not entertained, and they do not come back easily.

The most important problem a webtoon platform must solve is how to secure varied, high-quality webtoons on a continuing basis.

A webtoon is a labor-intensive product requiring both the creativity to devise a story and the labor to render it in pictures; webtoon creators spend an average of 10.5 hours a day creating, over an average of 5.8 days a week. For a creator to keep serializing, creative work alone must support a living — and if webtoons can earn a high income, supply and demand naturally increase the number of creators. The resulting cycle — creator income ↑ → creators ↑ → varied, quality works ↑ → readers ↑ → platform revenue ↑ → creator income ↑ — is the basic principle of the webtoon platform business: the flywheel.

4.2 First push: the Challenge Comics system and fixed fees (base pay)

In 2006 Naver created "Challenge Manga", a section where amateur creators could freely post their work, and secured a large body of quality work in a short period by discovering and debuting proven amateurs as professional creators. Unlike print comics, where the mainstream route was an apprenticeship of several years under a famous artist, every amateur's work was judged directly by the end consumer — the reader — and works that drew a good response were promoted to official serialization. As of June 2014, 139,789 people had posted work on Naver's Challenge Manga, of whom 175 made an official debut, accounting for about half (47.9%) of the 365 officially serialized creators at the time. The Sound of Your Heart (7 billion cumulative views) by Jo Seok and Tower of God (4.5 billion views) by Lee Jong-hui (pen name SIU) were discovered this way.

On top of this, portals signed fixed-fee contracts with officially serialized creators, paying a set monthly amount regardless of a work's popularity. In 2009 a new creator's monthly fee was KRW 400,000–800,000, barely above the minimum cost of living at the time (KRW 490,000 for a single-person household), but as webtoon traffic grew it rose 2.5–3 times to KRW 1.2–2 million by 2014, making a more stable life possible even on a once-weekly serialization.

4.3 Second push: revenue models and revenue sharing (performance pay)

The partial-payment revenue model introduced in earnest from 2013, together with a range of webtoon advertising formats, opened the way for creators to earn high incomes based on performance. Creators received not only a flat fee but a share of the paid transactions on their own webtoons; at Naver and Kakao the split between creator and platform is reported to range from 5.5:4.5 to 9:1. Just before introducing partial monetization, Naver unveiled its Page Profit Sharing (PPS) program, which shares advertising revenue from webtoon pages with creators, and in June 2013 reported that creators' average monthly PPS income was KRW 2.55 million. Park Tae-jun, creator of the popular webtoon Lookism, said he earned KRW 10 million a month from webtoon advertising alone. Simulating monthly revenue-share income for a work averaging 500,000 views per episode gives roughly KRW 18 million. In 2019 Naver reported that the average annual income across all creators on its platform was KRW 310 million, that new creators (within a year of serialization) earned KRW 160 million, and that 221 creators — 62% of the total — earned more than KRW 100 million a year.

4.4 Third push: IP business and royalties (+α)

As quality works accumulated on webtoon platforms, attempts naturally followed to produce secondary works based on webtoons with proven appeal, and the platforms recognized the value and entered the IP business directly. In return for licensing the creation of secondary works, creators receive royalties — new income (+α) requiring no additional labor. The success of a secondary work in turn raises views of the original webtoon. The popular webtoon Itaewon Class finished serialization in August 2018 with 250 million cumulative views and 7 million cumulative subscribers; after the 2020 drama adaptation ended its successful run with a 17% rating for the final episode, cumulative views and subscribers doubled to 400 million and 20 million respectively, and cumulative revenue is reported to have risen by more than 300%. In 2019 the top 20 creators serializing on Naver averaged KRW 1.75 billion in income including revenue from secondary works, and in 2021 Naver Webtoon disclosed that its highest-earning creator made KRW 12.4 billion a year.

4.5 Verifying the flywheel: growth in creators, works, readers and platform revenue

The data support the flywheel. New webtoons, which had fluctuated below 50 a year in 2003–2006, grew at an annual average of 41.7% from 2006 (2006–2012) after Naver put the Challenge Comics system into full operation; in 2013, when paid revenue sharing and advertising revenue sharing were introduced, the number of new works doubled to 382 from 194 the previous year. Steep growth continued at an annual average of 72.8% through 2016. The number of new creators follows a similar trajectory. Each time a structure was added that could raise a creator's expected income, the number of webtoon creators and works turned upward together at an inflection point.

Paradoxically, the importance of the flywheel was also proved in a crisis. From October 2016, the pirate site Bamtoki illegally hosted some 90,000 Korean webtoons; as reader traffic and paid transactions on legitimate platforms fell noticeably, the number of new works and creators dropped sharply in 2017. While Bamtoki's traffic grew 145%, page views at Naver and Kakao (Daum) fell 43% and 81% respectively, and one creator reported that monthly revenue fell from KRW 14 million at the start of serialization to KRW 2.5 million. The episode shows how important guaranteeing creator income is to securing creators and quality work in numbers.

Readers and platform revenue grew together as well. Naver Webtoon's monthly active users rose fourteen-fold, from 5 million in 2010 to 72 million worldwide in 2020, and in 2020 Naver's webtoon revenue reached KRW 360.9 billion (6.8% of total revenue) and Kakao's KRW 528 billion (12.7%) — establishing webtoons as a core business for both companies.

5. Global strategy

Building on a webtoon business model proven at home, Naver and Kakao moved quickly into global markets, creating and pre-empting a new webtoon market of their own making. Three strategies were designed to reinforce first-mover advantage and cross-side network effects. First, rapid global entry. In 2014, immediately after partial monetization established the business model — the moment it was confirmed that webtoon platforms could make money — Naver entered overseas markets without delay, giving powerful global platforms no opening. Second, active reuse of what had worked at home. CANVAS, the overseas version of Challenge Comics, receives around 1,000 episode uploads a day, and as of 2020 some 580,000 amateur creators and about 1,600 professional creators worldwide were active on it. Kakao, which entered Japan with Piccoma in 2016, transplanted the "Wait Until Free" model unchanged and by 2018 had overtaken Naver's Line Manga, which had entered earlier, to take first place. Third, new pushes designed for global markets: translating popular Korean webtoons into local languages (True Beauty is offered in eight languages and ranks among the most popular titles in several countries) and acquiring local platforms — Wattpad (Naver, January 2021, North America's leading web novel platform), Tapas and Radish (Kakao, 2021) — to secure readers and works quickly.

The results are clear. Naver Webtoon, offered in ten languages, has held first place among mobile comics apps in more than 100 countries since 2019 and in the third quarter of 2021 passed KRW 100 billion in average monthly global transaction value for the first time; Marvel and DC agreed to serialize original content for Black Widow, Shang-Chi and Batman on Naver Webtoon even though both have their own dedicated platforms. Kakao Piccoma ranked sixth worldwide in non-game app revenue in the third quarter of 2021 — the only Korean service in the top ten, alongside TikTok, YouTube and Disney+.

6. Summary, contribution and limitations

To secure a steady supply of webtoons — a labor-intensive product — the platforms built a flywheel that raises creator income so that good creators keep coming, which in turn secures quality work. The webtoon business then powered itself through a flywheel that never stopped turning.
Implications
  • Creator income is the heart of the flywheel: Business model innovations introduced in stages — the Challenge Comics system, the partial-payment revenue model and IP business — raised creator income through a structure of base pay plus performance pay plus α, and this was the first step in the chain reaction that keeps good creators and quality works flowing.
  • Global potential confirmed: By entering early to pre-empt the market, transplanting what had worked at home, and adding new pushes in local-language translation and acquisitions of local platforms, Naver took first place among comics apps in more than 100 countries and Kakao Piccoma reached sixth worldwide in non-game app revenue.
  • Contribution: The webtoon business is a meaningful case showing that Korean platform businesses, once confined to the domestic market, can compete globally — and that beyond the one-off success of individual content, an industry ecosystem can be built on a platform foundation.
  • Limitations: Year-by-year data on creators, works, readers and creator income for each individual platform could not be obtained; with such data the effectiveness of the flywheel could have been examined more closely.
