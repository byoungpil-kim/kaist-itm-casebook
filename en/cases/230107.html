Innovation Attributes and Technological Knowledge Influence by Firm Type in the Semiconductor Equipment Industry

Combine a management-of-technology theme with an industry to see cases that match both.

As customers demand ever higher semiconductor quality in the Fourth Industrial Revolution, semiconductor equipment grows more important. Research on semiconductor equipment, however, has been dominated by management case studies, leaving work from a technology and innovation perspective very thin. This study addresses the question "What are the innovation attributes and the factors behind technological knowledge influence, by firm type, among semiconductor equipment companies?" It divides six semiconductor front-end equipment firms into an integrated equipment group and a single-equipment group and quantitatively analyses patents registered over 20 years.

Research summary
  • As demand for semiconductor quality rises in the Fourth Industrial Revolution, semiconductor equipment grows more important, yet existing research has been dominated by management case studies with little work from a technology and innovation perspective.
  • This study divides six semiconductor front-end equipment firms into an integrated equipment group and a single-equipment group and analyses patents registered over 20 years, drawing on the characteristics of semiconductor equipment as a specialised-supplier industry and a complex product system.
  • It finds that the integrated equipment group has higher technological knowledge linkage than the single-equipment group, while the single-equipment group has higher technological knowledge influence than the integrated group.
  • For both groups, open innovation has a negative effect and technological convergence a positive effect on technological knowledge influence.
  • The contribution of the study lies in broadening understanding of the semiconductor equipment industry and offering a new perspective for corporate strategy.

1. Introduction

The core technologies of the Fourth Industrial Revolution — autonomous vehicles, the Internet of Things, the metaverse — are inseparable from semiconductors, which also bear significantly on military competitiveness and on national and international security. The semiconductor industry is equipment-intensive: the advanced technological knowledge of the equipment makers (OEMs) that build the tools for fabricating devices is indispensable. However brilliant a design may be, without equipment able to process the wafer accordingly no device can be produced. Evidence came in October 2022 with the US export controls on advanced semiconductor equipment to China; experts expect these restrictions, which cut China off from the equipment of firms with high technological knowledge, to set back Chinese semiconductor technology. Because equipment makers' technological knowledge bears directly on the device quality of their customers — integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) and foundries — advancing the semiconductor industry requires first examining the types and attributes of equipment makers' technological knowledge.

2. Theoretical background

2.1 Semiconductor equipment technology

The performance of a semiconductor device is determined by integration density and aspect ratio, and producing high-performance devices demands highly difficult technological knowledge in lithography, etching and deposition equipment. Lithography equipment has advanced by raising resolution with shorter-wavelength light sources, arriving at extreme ultraviolet (EUV) tools; etching equipment is pushing past the limits of dry etching with cryogenic etch; and in deposition, atomic layer deposition (ALD) equipment, which deposits material one atomic layer at a time, is being applied in volume production.

2.2 Innovation attributes of semiconductor equipment technology

Under Pavitt's (1984) taxonomy of innovation, the semiconductor equipment industry belongs to the specialised supplier category, sensitive to product performance rather than price, and has therefore concentrated on technological knowledge that raises equipment performance rather than on cutting prices. Semiconductor equipment is also classified as a complex product system (CoPS), distinct from mass-produced goods (MPG). Here "complex" means that the components and subsystems rest on diverse technological bases, and "system" means that integrating them and making them interact is what matters. As a complex product system, semiconductor equipment has two features. First, firms actively pursue open innovation, collaborating with internal and external innovation participants to cover diverse technological bases. Second, technological convergence is required to integrate externally obtained technology with internal technology into a single system. The lithography maker ASML, which turns externally supplied light sources and lenses into the converged system that is a lithography tool, is the archetypal case.

2.3 Types of semiconductor equipment firms

Semiconductor equipment divides into front-end-of-line (FEOL) and back-end-of-line (BEOL) tools. Front-end equipment, which bears heavily on overall device performance, is the more important and has higher barriers to entry, so a small number of global firms dominate the market. Front-end equipment firms divide further into an integrated equipment group making tools for two or more process types (Applied Materials, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron) and a single-equipment group specialising in tools for one process (ASML in lithography, ASM in deposition, KLA in metrology).

3. Hypotheses and method

Drawing on the three characteristics of semiconductor equipment — specialised supplier, open innovation and technological convergence — the study sets four hypotheses. H1: the integrated equipment group has higher technological knowledge linkage than the single-equipment group. H2: the single-equipment group has higher technological knowledge influence than the integrated group. H3: open innovation has a positive effect on technological knowledge influence in both groups. H4: the degree of technological convergence has a positive effect on technological knowledge influence in both groups.

The data comprise the 28,366 registered patents among 54,477 US patent applications filed over the 20 years from 2000 to 2019 by three integrated equipment firms and three single-equipment firms drawn from the leading companies by 2020 revenue. The variables were operationalised as follows. Open innovation is a dummy variable based on whether a patent was co-filed (two or more applicants). Technological convergence is measured by the number of distinct IPC codes assigned to a patent; because looking only at the subclass level (four characters) would distort the detail — every patent would classify as H01L — IPC codes down to main group and subgroup were used. Technological knowledge influence is measured by forward citations, and technological knowledge linkage by an index based on the number of overlapping patents within a group (0 = no linkage, values closer to 1 indicating higher linkage).

4. Results

4.1 Technological knowledge linkage by firm type (H1 supported)

Because firms in the integrated equipment group produce the same kinds of tools alongside one another, the technological knowledge needed to develop them overlaps. Aggregated across groups, technological knowledge linkage was 0.73 for the integrated equipment group against 0.37 for the single-equipment group — about twice as high — supporting H1. The most overlapping IPC codes in the integrated group were deposition equipment (C23C-016/00), etching equipment (H01L-021/302), semiconductor equipment overall (H01L-021/00) and plasma-related (H01J-037/32), with patents for various tool types distributed evenly across the firms.

4.2 Technological knowledge influence by firm type (H2 supported)

Firms in the single-equipment group each hold a high market share in their own equipment field. ASML has built a monopoly with up to 90% of the lithography market; KLA holds 58% of the metrology market — to the point that KLARF, the defect analysis file format, is named after the company; and ASM has achieved 52% in ALD, the field with the most demanding quality requirements. A t-test showed mean forward citations of 30.62 for the single-equipment group against 23.91 for the integrated group (difference 6.71, t = 8.4376), supporting H2. This indicates that the single-equipment group's high market share rests less on other factors than on the concentrated accumulation of technological knowledge in a single tool.

The single-equipment group's high market share rests, more than on any other factor, on the concentrated accumulation of technological knowledge in a single tool.

4.3 The effects of open innovation and technological convergence (H3 rejected, H4 supported)

Because the dependent variable, forward citations, was overdispersed (variance greater than mean), negative binomial regression was used instead of Poisson regression. The coefficient on the open innovation variable was negative in both groups, so H3 — that open innovation has a positive effect on technological knowledge influence — was not supported (integrated −0.479, single −0.728, both p<0.01). The coefficient on the convergence variable, by contrast, was positive in both groups, supporting H4.

Implications
  • Opposing strengths by type: Analysis of 28,366 patents registered over 20 years shows that the integrated equipment group has high technological knowledge linkage (an overlapping knowledge base) while the single-equipment group has high technological knowledge influence (concentrated knowledge accumulation). The single-equipment group's high market share in particular derives from concentrated accumulation of technological knowledge in one tool.
  • The positive effect of technological convergence: In both groups, the more frequent the technological convergence the greater the technological knowledge influence. Convergence acts as a moderating factor in advancing the technological knowledge of semiconductor equipment as a complex product system.
  • An unexpected result on open innovation: Open innovation based on collaboration (co-filing) had a negative effect on technological knowledge influence in both groups, contrary to expectation, and the hypothesis was not supported. This result warrants further research.
  • Contribution and limitations: The study extends semiconductor equipment research beyond qualitative case studies to a quantitative analysis of 20 years of accumulated patents, offering objective findings for corporate strategy and government policy. Limiting the analysis to patents, however, meant no relationship with financial performance could be drawn, and because equipment makers often reinforce appropriability through undocumented information such as trade secrets and know-how, public information alone has its limits.
