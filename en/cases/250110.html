A Latecomer SME's Entry into a Global Oligopoly: New-Market Disruptive Innovation at i-SENS

Within medical devices, the blood glucose monitoring market has long been an oligopoly of global corporations such as Roche, Abbott and Dexcom, protected by high technological barriers to entry. Using appropriability theory and new-market disruptive innovation as its analytical framework, this study analyses the strategy through which i-SENS, a Korean SME, entered that market and secured competitiveness.

Research summary
  • Amid the shift to digital healthcare and rising demand for personal health management, the blood glucose monitoring market has formed an oligopoly of global corporations behind high technological barriers to entry. This study analyses i-SENS's market entry and competitiveness strategy using appropriability theory and new-market disruptive innovation as its framework.
  • i-SENS accumulated high-precision sensor technology through sustained R&D and secured appropriability by connecting it to patents and to an internalisation strategy in production and distribution.
  • At the same time it launched products differentiated from existing high-priced offerings on price competitiveness and technological excellence, creating new demand.
  • Combined with diversified production methods, quality certifications and expanded production bases, this strategy became the foundation for global expansion.
  • The case empirically demonstrates how a latecomer SME used technological appropriability and disruptive innovation to enter an oligopolistic market and expand globally, and offers strategic implications.

1. Introduction

Ageing is proceeding rapidly worldwide and the prevalence of chronic disease is rising with it. CES 2025 named digital health one of the key technologies of the year. Diabetes is a representative chronic disease, and among in-vitro diagnostic devices the blood glucose meter is not only essential to managing it but is also drawing interest among general consumers as wellness trends spread; personal blood glucose test strips are among Korea's top two medical device exports. Yet the blood glucose monitoring market has long been an oligopoly of global corporations, a structure in which it is very hard for an SME to enter and succeed.

2. Background and purpose

The personal blood glucose monitoring industry falls, in Pavitt's (1984) classification, into the science-based category characterised by knowledge accumulated through internal R&D and patent-centred appropriability strategies, and it has the structural feature that high technological barriers and strong consumer lock-in limit new entry. The global blood glucose monitoring market, worth about USD 17 billion in 2023, is projected to grow about 9% a year to about USD 33 billion by 2030. Technologically it divides into blood glucose monitoring (BGM), based on a fingertip blood sample, and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), which measures glucose concentration in interstitial fluid around the clock through a skin-mounted sensor; a technological paradigm shift is under way within the industry as the centre of gravity moves from BGM to CGM.

3. The company: i-SENS

i-SENS was founded in 2000 by Cha Keun-sik and Nam Hak-hyun, both formerly professors at Kwangwoon University, and produces and sells point-of-care testing (POCT) medical devices including blood glucose meters, built on electrochemical and biosensor technology. Its 2024 revenue was KRW 291.1 billion, with blood glucose monitoring accounting for about 85% of the total. It launched its first blood glucose meter, the CareSens series, in 2003, obtained CE certification and ISO 13485 in 2004, and completed its Giheung plant to establish volume production. In 2012 it was selected as the exclusive supplier of self-monitoring blood glucose meters to the New Zealand government, and from the mid-2010s it took first place by share in Korea's blood glucose monitoring market. It listed on KOSDAQ in 2013, and in 2023 launched the CGM device CareSens Air while acquiring the US CGM specialist AgaMatrix. Its core capabilities are R&D-based technological appropriability and strategic commercialisation capability, meeting all three of Prahalad and Hamel's (1990) conditions: market access, inimitability and customer value.

4. Theoretical background

4.1 Disruptive innovation

Christensen and Raynor (2003) distinguished two forms of disruptive innovation. One is low-end disruption, aimed at consumers not satisfied by the over-performance of existing products; the other is new-market disruption, which creates new demand among non-consumers who could not access the market because existing products were too expensive or too complex. Christensen et al. (2015) redefined disruptive innovation as being characterised not by technological superiority but by a path that starts at the margins and encroaches on the mainstream in ways incumbents find hard to answer.

4.2 Appropriability

Teece (1986) showed that the firm leading a technological innovation does not necessarily capture the economic returns, which often accrue instead to imitators or to firms holding complementary assets. He identified the appropriability regime, complementary assets and the dominant design as the determinants of monetisation, and proposed that a firm choose the most effective commercialisation strategy among integration, collaboration and licensing. Empirical work by Levin et al. (1987) and Cohen et al. (2000) shows that patents are an effective means of appropriation particularly in industries such as pharmaceuticals and chemicals, and that various means of appropriation work in combination and differ by industry.

5. Case study

5.1 R&D as the core strategy

The founders led a joint chemical sensor research group at their university and were selected for a government basic science research support programme in 1995, which led them to begin developing electrochemical sensor technology with commercial applications. On founding the company they established an in-house research institute divided into a sensor institute and a device institute; as of 2024 more than 15% of the workforce is in research and R&D investment is held above about 10% of revenue (10.6% in 2024). In a comparative study of BGM accuracy published in Australia, CareSens was reported as the only product meeting the American Diabetes Association's recommended bias criterion of under ±5%.

In its early years the company pursued commercialisation through technology licensing, lacking capital and complementary assets such as manufacturing and distribution infrastructure. Once the precision and reliability of its technology were proven and a revenue base secured, it launched the CareSens series under its own brand in 2003 and shifted to a business model of manufacturing and selling directly. This is an integration strategy that captures the returns on technological innovation within the firm. On the patent side, as of 2024 it holds 443 registered patents at home and abroad, with 692 patent actions in progress including 242 overseas registrations and 450 applications relating to blood glucose monitoring. Internalising the manufacturing process acts as a form of protection through tacit knowledge, making imitation difficult for competitors and raising switching costs to induce customer lock-in.

5.2 BGM innovation and commercialisation

At the company's founding, most BGM devices required 3–6 μL of blood and more than 30 seconds to produce a reading, and the total cost of ownership — with repeated strip purchases pushing annual costs above KRW 1 million at five to ten measurements a day — held back the spread of self-monitoring. i-SENS became the first in Korea to commercialise technology measuring within five seconds from only about 0.5 μL of blood, and by improving price accessibility drew into the market a latent demand — elderly and non-expert users, among others — that had avoided self-monitoring for technical and economic reasons. As a result, from 2017 it overtook the global leader Roche to become the top company by revenue in Korea's blood glucose monitoring market, expanding its share from 20% in 2014 to 36% in 2019.

Entering global markets with limited brand recognition, it adopted a strategy running OEM/ODM alongside its own brand. About half of total production volume is OEM/ODM and the rest own-brand, and the company has established local subsidiaries and production bases in major overseas markets including the United States, Germany and China. Because the structure of the BGM market makes repeat strip purchases the heart of profitability, this high-volume production and supply system was decisive in securing lock-in and long-term profitability.

5.3 CGM innovation and commercialisation

The global CGM market, worth about USD 8.9 billion in 2023, is expected to grow 16.5% a year through 2030, but high barriers — precision sensors, complex algorithms and regulatory approval — meant only a very few corporations such as Dexcom, Abbott and Medtronic had succeeded in entering, and high prices confined demand to patients with severe diabetes. In September 2023 i-SENS launched CareSens Air, the first CGM commercialised in Korea. At launch its mean absolute relative difference (MARD) was about 9.8%, comparable to global leaders such as the Dexcom G6 (about 9.8%) and Abbott FreeStyle Libre (about 9.4%), and it improved usability with an integrated sensor-transmitter design dispensing with a separate transmitter, automatic Bluetooth transmission, and a 15-day sensor life — among the longest in the industry. Platform integration with Kakao Healthcare and Samsung Health used digital platforms it does not own as external complementary assets, delivering everyday usability that medically focused CGM had not offered, and created a new market category by drawing in non-consumers such as patients with mild diabetes and general health-management users.

On certification, i-SENS obtained EU CE marking in February 2024, ahead of Roche, the global BGM leader (July 2024), the first Korean company to obtain CE certification for a CGM. Domestically it is the only Korean company to have obtained Ministry of Food and Drug Safety approval for a continuous glucose monitoring system, and it is preparing for US FDA certification while participating in a cross-ministerial full-cycle medical device R&D programme. In the medical device industry, certifications such as CE and FDA function as core complementary assets enabling market entry and expansion.

New-market disruptive innovation cannot be achieved through a simply cheap, low-performance product; it becomes possible only when a certain level of technical precision and user accessibility are met at the same time.

6. Conclusion

From the perspective of appropriability theory, i-SENS appropriated its R&D-based core technology principally through patents to prevent imitation, and internalised the returns on technological innovation by shifting from a revenue structure centred on technology licensing to own-brand production and distribution. From the perspective of new-market disruptive innovation, it converted consumers who had avoided self-monitoring into real demand in the BGM market with products improving usability and price accessibility, and in the CGM market it secured precision and convenience while easing the cost barrier, newly encompassing users with general health-management goals. Combined with process innovation and a certification strategy, these two strategies effectively overcame the structural barriers to entering global markets.

Implications
  • Combining technological appropriability with commercialisation capability: A firm should not stop at securing core technology but protect it through varied means of appropriation — patents, production infrastructure, brand — and connect them to an internalisation strategy that does not depend on outsiders to capture the returns.
  • The conditions for new-market disruptive innovation: Not a simply cheap, low-performance product, but one meeting a certain level of technical precision and user accessibility at once, realised through absorbing non-consumers and creating new contexts of use.
  • Internalising regulatory response: Reflecting global certification standards in the design from the R&D planning stage allowed the company to obtain demanding quality certifications relatively quickly. In industries where certification and regulation are the key gateway to commercialisation, the capability to address the regulatory environment from the earliest design stage can be central to competitiveness.
  • The strategic value of basic research: The precision and consistency of sensor technology secured through basic research carried through BGM productisation into the technological transition to CGM and market expansion.
  • Limitations: Long-term analysis of the CGM launched in 2023 is limited, generalisability is constrained by the single-firm case, and external institutional factors such as regulation and insurance systems were outside the scope of the analysis.
