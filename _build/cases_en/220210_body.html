How did HD Hyundai Heavy Industries, a latecomer entering a conservative engine market with high barriers to entry, manage to build its own model — the HiMSEN engine — and go on to become the world's number one in medium-speed engines? This study examines the innovation factors behind the company's engine and machinery business, using as its main analytical framework the technological capability accumulation process of firms in developing countries (Kim Wang-dong, 2002).

Research summary
  • The study examines how HD Hyundai Heavy Industries, a latecomer entering a conservative engine market with high barriers to entry, was able to build its own HiMSEN engine, and how it went on to become number one in the world in the medium-speed engine market.
  • The main analytical framework is the technological capability accumulation process of firms in developing countries.
  • The key factors in the company's innovation include improvement-led design (Hi Touch) and the development of new dual-fuel engines.
  • The driving forces behind those factors were accumulated production experience, sustained R&D and the "Hyundai spirit."

1. Background and purpose

On a separate-entity basis in 2021, HD Hyundai Heavy Industries recorded total revenue of KRW 8.3 trillion: shipbuilding KRW 6.3 trillion (77%), engines and machinery KRW 1.5 trillion (18%) and offshore plant KRW 0.4 trillion (5%). Among Korea's "big three" shipbuilders — Hyundai Heavy Industries, Daewoo Shipbuilding and Samsung Heavy Industries — only Hyundai has an engine and machinery business. While shipbuilding ran losses for several years beginning with a deficit of more than KRW 1.8 trillion in 2014, the engine and machinery business stayed profitable throughout except in 2014 (a KRW 10.9 billion loss). Of the 23 world-class products the company holds — the most of any Korean firm — 12 come from the engine division, more than shipbuilding's 10.

Marine engines are a core piece of shipbuilding equipment accounting for about 10% of a vessel's price. They are (1) built to order and specified by the shipowner, which makes standardization difficult across vessel types and hull forms; (2) subject to demanding reliability requirements such as durability and stability; and (3) long-lead-time items requiring six to nine months from production to installation. In short, the engine market is hard to enter. In that market, Hyundai Heavy Industries — a licensee that had been mass-producing under technology agreements with European technology firms — independently developed the HiMSEN engine, Korea's only proprietary engine brand, and entered the medium-speed engine market.

2. Theoretical background: competitive strategy for latecomers

Schnaars (1994) proposed low price, imitate-then-improve, and high quality with market power as competitive strategies for latecomers, while Golder and Tellis (1996) identified early entry into the mass market, managerial persistence, investment, sustained innovation and asset leverage as factors behind late-mover market leadership. The main framework of this study, Kim Wang-dong's (2002) model of technological capability accumulation in developing-country firms, holds that external environmental factors influence internal and external technology sources and technology strategy, and that an organization accumulates technology by learning through processes of observation, evaluation, design and execution. Accumulated technological capability then feeds back into internal technology sources and technology strategy in a cycling process. The theory of technology development by industry stage (Lee Jin-joo, Bae Jong-tae et al., 1988) serves as a secondary framework.

3. Industry and company analysis

Marine engines divide into two-stroke engines (low speed; technology leader MAN), used as the main engine of large vessels, and four-stroke engines (medium speed; technology leader Wärtsilä), used as auxiliary engines for power generation. Hyundai Heavy Industries sells large engines for ship propulsion and medium-speed engines for shipboard power generation (the HiMSEN engine) as its main products, and as of 2021 held the world's top share in both: 30% in large engines and 25% in medium-speed engines. Against domestic competitors, its maximum engine output capacity of 16 million horsepower is more than three times that of HSD Engine and STX Engine (4.3–5 million horsepower), and its 2021 operating margin of 8.3% contrasts with a loss at HSD Engine and a slim profit at STX Engine.

The engine business can be divided into a founding period up to the 2001 launch of its proprietary model, a growth period running through the dual-fuel launch and the shipbuilding boom, and a maturity period continuing to the present. Revenue stayed at around KRW 500 billion in the founding period, but in the growth period it rose to KRW 3 trillion as the invested production plants came on line, order intake grew with the shipbuilding boom, and the proprietary medium-speed engine was developed and mass-produced. In the maturity period, as environmental regulation shifts engine fuel from diesel to cleaner alternatives, the ability to develop eco-friendly engines is becoming the decisive fork in the road.

4. Innovation case study

4.1 (Founding period / RQ1) Why the latecomer succeeded in developing its own engine

Accumulated production experience. Hyundai Heavy Industries built production experience while securing a stable captive market within the group (Hyundai Mipo Dockyard and Hyundai Samho Heavy Industries). After producing its first large diesel engine in 1979, it reached 30 million cumulative horsepower in 2001 — becoming the world's largest producer — and 50 million horsepower in 2005, a world first. Receiving basic designs from licensors while producing more units than anyone else in the world allowed it to deepen its capability in production design and in production and assembly technology.

Improvement-led design (Hi Touch). Once competition over fuel savings settled down after the 1970s oil shocks, customer requirements began to shift toward longer maintenance intervals, lower and easier maintenance costs and higher reliability. Through its manufacturing experience Hyundai Heavy Industries read this change — that customers wanted products that were easy to use more than high-performance ones — and on that basis developed a proprietary model built on a Hi Touch concept (simple, robust, smart) differentiated from the Hi Tech (high-performance, cutting-edge, expensive) character of existing engines. Its essentials were a simplified structure, a 30% reduction in the number of parts, and serviceability allowing maintenance with a single-stage teardown. In architectural terms (Fujimoto, 2001) a marine engine has a closed-integral structure, and Hi Touch improved the model by reducing that integral complexity. From an absorptive capacity perspective, deep-rooted production skill — production workers make up 60% of the workforce (7,000 people), and the company has produced 29 Korea Masters as of 2017, the most in the industry — enabled a virtuous cycle in which technology absorbed and improved on the shop floor was passed back to and absorbed by design and research staff.

Collaboration between the research center and design. Proprietary engine development was pursued over a target period of eight years, in two stages of technology development and product development, and along two tracks: a development department (product development) and an in-house research center working with universities (basic research). This corresponds to the shared-division-with-overlap type described by Takeuchi and Nonaka (1986); when decision-making began to slow, the company resolved it by seconding lead researchers from the research center to the development department for extended periods. After forming an engine development task force centered on design staff in 1992, starting single-cylinder engine development in 1994 and adopting the Hi Touch concept in 1998, the HiMSEN engine passed its test run at 3:30 a.m. in July 2000, and in 2001 cleared classification society performance tests and a review by the existing licensor (MAN) for any infringement of its technology. Even so, in an engine market where reliability is paramount, no customer was eager to buy a new product; the first sale came in October 2001 to the Dominican Republic, on terms extending the warranty by a year (to two) and offering 100% compensation in the event of performance problems. Quality was subsequently recognized, and in 2005 the company signed a major contract to supply HiMSEN engines for a portable power station (PPS) to Cuba for USD 460 million.

The HiMSEN engine's technology is recognized worldwide — to the point that the engine appears on Cuban currency.

The Hyundai spirit (management philosophy). Lacking its own technology, Hyundai Heavy Industries not only paid around KRW 10 billion a year in license royalties but also saw a large engine export contract canceled in 1990 when its technology partner withheld approval. As the need for proprietary technology grew under the owner management of president Chung Mong-joon and the technology-centered management of president Min Kye-sik, who came from the research center, a long-term project was approved in 1993 — one that carried the risk of severing the existing technology agreement, and that in the end took seven years and KRW 40 billion in R&D spending. The source of that drive can be found in the management philosophy grounded in founder Chung Ju-yung's thinking: the "Hyundai spirit" of creative foresight, positive will and tenacious drive. One result came in November 2020, when the HD Hyundai group and Saudi Aramco established SEMCO, a joint venture to build around 200 engines a year; SEMCO will produce HiMSEN engines and marine pumps under license from Hyundai Heavy Industries — a landmark shift from licensee to licensor.

4.2 (Growth and maturity periods / RQ2) Why the latecomer succeeded in expanding the market

Investment in R&D and production technology. R&D spending rose steadily from KRW 77.8 billion in 1999 to KRW 184.1 billion in 2010, then fell to KRW 90.7 billion in 2017 and KRW 70.8 billion in 2018 following the large-scale restructuring of 2016 (a KRW 3.5 trillion self-rescue plan) prompted by the shipbuilding downturn. Instead, investment shifted from quantity to quality and selectivity, concentrating on future core technologies such as eco-friendly engines and a wider range of engine outputs. The results included the world's first high-pressure exhaust gas cleaning system (2016) and the launch of the world's highest-output dual-fuel HiMSEN engine, the H54DF (2019). Marine engine patents (710 in total) likewise rose to 93 filings in 2015 before declining, though filings on core technologies continued. The company also kept investing in production skills training, winning gold medals at the WorldSkills Competition for 20 consecutive editions from 1983, with 110 entrants and 105 medals (51 gold).

Launching a new (dual-fuel) engine. In 2012 the company developed the world's first dual-fuel package, linking a large dual-fuel engine (co-developed with MAN), a dual-fuel HiMSEN engine (developed in-house) and an LNG fuel gas system (also developed in-house). A dual-fuel engine can switch freely between diesel and gas, cutting emissions of carbon, nitrogen and sulphur oxides while delivering the same performance as a diesel engine. The result was a striking outcome: in 2021, 85% of the medium-speed dual-fuel engine market for LNG vessels used the HiMSEN H35DF.

Facility investment and future eco-friendly engines. Engine plant capacity expanded more than fivefold, from 3 million horsepower in 1999 to 16 million today, and investment of more than KRW 250 billion secured price competitiveness through economies of scale relative to competitors. In September 2021 the company became the first in the industry to obtain approval in principle (AIP) from the Korean Register for the conceptual design of an eco-friendly ammonia fuel supply system, and it is now working toward a methanol engine in 2022, an ammonia engine in 2023 and a hydrogen engine in 2025. With the dominant design shifting from diesel engines to dual-fuel engines (LNG cuts carbon dioxide by 23% against heavy fuel oil) and on to next-generation clean engines (methanol cuts carbon by 95%; ammonia and hydrogen emit no carbon), the present moment can be read in double S-curve terms as the transition to a new S-curve.

4.3 Applying the framework (technological capability accumulation)

Changes in the external environment such as high royalties and restrictions on sales, together with external factors (competitors, users) and internal factors (production experience and technology), shaped technology strategy. Organizational factors — the chief executive's active support, collaboration between design and the research center, and the Hyundai spirit — further influenced both the technology strategy of developing a proprietary and then a dual-fuel engine and the technological learning activities behind it. The technological capability so acquired feeds back into organizational factors and internal technology, creating a virtuous cycle that carries on into the development of future eco-friendly engines.

Implications
  • Why proprietary engine development succeeded (RQ1): Accumulated production experience, improvement-led design (Hi Touch), collaboration between the research center and design, and the Hyundai spirit as a management philosophy were the key factors from the founding through the growth period.
  • Why market expansion succeeded (RQ2): Investment in R&D and production technology, the launch of the new dual-fuel engine, facility investment, and the development of future eco-friendly engines (methanol, ammonia and hydrogen) were the key factors from the growth through the maturity period.
  • Turning points and driving force: In the founding period the turning point was differentiation and improvement (Hi Touch) setting the product apart from existing engines; from the growth period onward it was the development of a new engine suited to an environmental era (the dual-fuel engine). The driving force behind those turning points lies in a management philosophy — the Hyundai spirit — in which technology absorbed through accumulated production experience feeds into R&D, and those research results become the basis for taking on something new again.
  • What remains: For large two-stroke engines, the conservatism of the market still makes proprietary development difficult (even SEMCO will license large engines from MAN and others), but on the strength of its proprietary medium-speed technology the company is participating in the joint development of first-of-class large engines running on new clean fuels.
