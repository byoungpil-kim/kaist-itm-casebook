Beginning with a KRW 4 trillion Cheongung-II contract with the United Arab Emirates in 2022, LIG Nex1 has exported precision-guided munitions (PGM) worth some KRW 12 trillion to three Middle Eastern countries, recording an all-time high order backlog of KRW 20,141.9 billion as of 2024. This case study analyses how LIG Nex1, once a latecomer, secured global competitiveness in advanced PGM across three axes — the firm, its supporting organisations and the institutional environment — and locates the underlying driver in Korea's distinctive state-led innovation system, a "Statist Triple Helix."

Research summary
  • LIG Nex1's success in exporting precision-guided munitions marks the inflection point at which Korea's defence industry made a qualitative shift from latecomer to a core actor in the global market, and its underlying driver lay beyond the firm's own R&D and business capability, in the structure of a Statist Triple Helix — a state-led innovation system.
  • In that structure, the Agency for Defense Development (ADD) monopolises knowledge production and ownership, an exemption from technology fees provides an institutional price advantage of more than 20% over European competitors, and integrated pan-government governance completes the export.
  • LIG's success is analysed as the successful realisation of a path-dependent strategy designed so that the state absorbs innovation risk in the name of national security while the private sector concentrates on volume production and exports.
  • As the future battlefield paradigm shifts, the present Statist Triple Helix is exposing its limits; sustaining innovation momentum will require structural reform — reintroducing universities into the knowledge space, securing openness in the ecosystem through standard software and AI architectures, and entrenching export finance and technology fee measures as long-term institutions.
  • Ultimately, Korea's defence industry faces the strategic task of using its PGM export success as a platform to move toward leading global all-domain mosaic warfare (ADMW) standards.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background — K-defence and LIG Nex1 as game changers

Korea's defence industry passed USD 17 billion in defence exports in 2022, an all-time high, and after the Russia–Ukraine war it ranked second in the world by defence orders, with an order backlog above KRW 75 trillion as of the first quarter of 2024 — a qualitative change in standing from a traditional arms importer (follower) to a core actor in the global market (leader). The study stresses, however, the need to distinguish clearly between "orders," reflecting the signing of large contracts, and "exports," recognised as actual revenue.

Within this rapid growth, LIG Nex1's PGM business is the core growth engine, accounting for 39.2% of total revenue as of 2024. From the roughly KRW 4 trillion Cheongung-II export to the UAE in 2022, it went on to large-scale exports of about KRW 12 trillion to three Middle Eastern countries, and its 2024 order backlog of KRW 20,141.9 billion is six times annual revenue. The study's starting point is that these results would have been impossible without the organic combination of a stable domestic market and export support provided by a national innovation system (NIS) centred on the Agency for Defense Development (ADD) and the Defense Acquisition Program Administration (DAPA).

1.2–1.3 Research questions and scope

The study identifies the factors behind LIG Nex1's PGM export success across three axes: the firm (R&D capability and business capability), supporting organisations (ADD, DAPA, KIDA) and the institutional environment (export incentives, pan-government governance). The repeated use of "latecomer" in the research questions signals the need to examine in depth the strategic choices by which a firm entering after incumbents have pre-empted the market and technology standards leapfrogs by avoiding their trial and error.

2. Theoretical background

2.1–2.2 Technological characteristics of the defence industry and inter-institutional relations

Weapons systems bear directly on mission success and operator survival, so they demand the highest reliability — a long-cycle industry converging many technologies. The Ministry of National Defense's ten strategic defence technology fields and the Ministry of Science and ICT's twelve national strategic technology fields overlap closely in AI, semiconductors and aerospace, showing the importance of dual-use technology through spin-in of civilian technology and spin-off of defence technology. Korea's defence industry has evolved into a tripartite structure of firms (technology development strategy), the military (requirements planning and feedback) and government (institutions and policy).

2.3 Applying innovation theory

LIG Nex1's success is not explained by any single theory. First, the national innovation system of Korean defence is an extreme form of the Statist Triple Helix, in which the role of the university in knowledge production within the standard triple helix model (university–industry–government) is replaced by ADD and by the military's verification of required operational capability (ROC). ADD monopolises the development and ownership of core technology while firms receive technology transfers (licences) and concentrate on volume production and exports, creating an institutional complementarity that minimises a latecomer's early R&D risk. Second, demand-pull innovation, in which the military's clearly specified ROC determines the direction of technology development, reduces R&D uncertainty, while rigorous test and evaluation — 67 verification items for Cheongung-II — produces the proven reliability of a weapon "fielded by the Korean military." Third, importing PGM systems through Russia's Bulgom programme and internalising them through teardown analysis was a leapfrogging strategy exploiting latecomer advantage; more recently the company has been shifting toward open innovation through the acquisition of Ghost Robotics and collaboration with Electroninks.

3. Case analysis

3.1 Company overview — a technology-centred firm with 59.1% of staff in R&D

Founded in 1976 as Goldstar Precision, LIG Nex1 has grown into a comprehensive defence company spanning precision-guided munitions (PGM), surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR), command, control and communications (C4I), and avionics and electronic warfare (AEW). Of about 6,200 employees, R&D staff accounted for 59.1% (3,217 people) as of the third quarter of 2024, and 58% of R&D staff hold a master's or doctoral degree. From developing the Shingung man-portable surface-to-air missile in 1997 and exporting the Haeseong missile to Latin America in 2004 — Korea's first guided weapon to enter the global market — the large Middle Eastern exports from 2022 formed the decisive inflection point of innovation. In core technology, Cheongung-II has hit-to-kill capability and manoeuvres at Mach 4.5–5, while Hyunmoo-4 reaches Mach 10 with CEP accuracy under 5 metres and penetration of more than 100 metres of concrete, four times that of the US GBU-28.

3.2 Success factor 1: the firm — a flywheel of R&D and business capability

Taking the military's clearly specified ROC as its control mechanism, LIG embodied a practical research culture placing operational usability first. Over ten years of Hyungung development from 2007, researchers met punishing ROCs including live firing tests in heavy snow at −30°C. It also systematically internalised the PGM systems imported through Russia's Bulgom programme (1995–2007), such as METIS-M and Igla, following Nonaka's SECI model: absorbing tacit knowledge such as design philosophy through exchange with Russian engineers (socialisation), codifying guidance and control principles into core algorithms through teardown analysis of the imported weapons (externalisation), integrating these with domestic electronic system specifications and component standards to complete a Korean technology standard (combination), and repeatedly applying and testing the fire-and-forget algorithm during Hyungung development to build operational proficiency (internalisation). As a result, localisation of the Shingung seeker rose above 95%.

On resources, LIG's R&D staff share of about 60% far exceeds that of major defence companies at home and abroad, such as Rafael (44%) and Lockheed Martin (30%). It is the only Korean firm holding KOLAS international accreditation across every field of reliability testing, and the only Korean defence company to have obtained CMMI Level 5, the highest rating in the US Department of Defense's international standard for R&D process capability. That certification is a strategic asset justifying a price premium of 15–20% on quality reliability. Patent filings over the past decade total about 2,515 — more than twice competitors' — with 58% concentrated in PGM-related IPC codes.

In business capability, customer-specific localisation stands out. For the UAE, LIG demonstrated interoperability with the Patriot air defence network already in place and positioned Cheongung-II as "part of the UAE's air defence," closing a KRW 2.6 trillion contract. For Saudi Arabia, it applied reinforced cooling systems and dust filters suited to desert heat above 50°C and sandstorms, cutting localisation time from the five years typical of US competitors to one. It invested more than KRW 300 billion in its Gumi and Gimcheon sites between 2022 and 2027 to expand production lines and produced key components ahead of contract signature, achieving 100% on-time delivery; and through ADD technology transfers and a vertically integrated supply chain of some 1,200 suppliers it reached 85% localisation, giving Hyungung a 30% price advantage over Javelin and Cheongung-II a 20% advantage over European competitors.

3.2.3 Success factor 2: supporting organisations — combat-proven reliability and integrated commercialisation support

Korean weapons acquisition passes three stages of verification: analytical experiments by the Joint Chiefs of Staff (military validity), technology development and preliminary research by ADD and the Defence Institute (technical validity), and test and evaluation by the Joint Chiefs (operational suitability). To meet the ROC of intercepting North Korean ballistic missiles, Cheongung-II passed 67 items of rigorous verification before deployment — a fact that functions for overseas customers as verified reliability, saving them substantial testing cost and time. Three further stages of integrated commercialisation support — requirements verification by KIDA, advance analysis of export potential by DAPA and the Defence Institute, and a one-team system led by the presidential office and DAPA with government-to-government diplomatic support — prevent the situation of "having the technology but being unable to sell it." When a supply chain conflict arose after the KRW 3.7 trillion Cheongung-II contract with Iraq in September 2024, with supplier Hanwha objecting on grounds of insufficient production capacity, DAPA activated the one-team system and mediated over ten months to stabilise the supply chain.

3.2.4 Success factor 3: the institutional environment — completing the institutional price advantage

On decades of institutional foundation — the creation of ADD (1970), the specialisation and systematisation scheme for guided weapons (1983) and the Bulgom programme (1995–2007) — the enactment of the Defense Science and Technology Innovation Act (2020) expanded a good-faith performance recognition scheme exempting firms from sanctions for technical failure, making it possible to take on high-risk next-generation PGM development. Above all, technology fees on defence exports were fully waived until the end of 2021 and the cumulative ceiling was lowered thereafter, effectively removing royalties on ADD-owned core technology (IP) and giving LIG's PGM an institutional price advantage. The expansion of the R&D tax credit rate for export-oriented defence R&D to 30% in 2024 further reduced the cost of customer-specific R&D, and a pyramid of integrated pan-government governance — a presidential defence export strategy meeting (top decision-making), a strategy assessment meeting chaired by the second deputy national security adviser (working-level coordination) and defence officers at the presidential office and overseas missions (field support) — completed a record order intake of some KRW 12 trillion.

3.3 Results of the innovation

Revenue in 2024 reached KRW 3,277 billion, up 42% on the previous year's KRW 2,308.6 billion, and operating profit rose 23.8% to KRW 230.8 billion. The share price and order backlog changed explosively at the turn of 2021 into 2022 — precisely when the UAE Cheongung-II export contract was signed. Fielding domestic PGMs such as Cheongung-II, L-SAM and Hyungung provided the core foundation of the Korea Air and Missile Defense (KAMD) system, and successful exports to three Middle Eastern countries became both the central driver of the "K-defence syndrome" and a bridgehead for building a K-defence belt.

4. Findings

4.1 Focus group interviews

Focus group interviews with 14 participants — LIG Nex1 staff, researchers at competitors (KAI and Hanwha), researchers at ADD and KRIT, analysts at DAPA and KIDA, academic experts and air force personnel with Cheongung-II operational experience — found that experts rated highly the company's ability to internalise advanced technology and to integrate systems on the strength of test facilities, while placing considerable weight on the fact that technology ownership is monopolised by ADD. Alongside an assessment that DAPA's role in mediating conflict was decisive to closing contracts, the view emerged that a long-term, predictable institutional support system unchanged by political transitions is needed.

4.2 Theoretical implications — an extreme form of the Statist Triple Helix

The innovation structure of Korean defence is characterised as an extreme form of the Statist Triple Helix, with an innovation system centred on national survival. The knowledge space (ADD) monopolises ownership of core technology and absorbs firms' early R&D risk; the innovation space (firms) specialises in volume production, systems integration and exports; and the consensus space (DAPA and the military) sets development direction through ROC and acts as the coordinating actor promoting exports. Seen through Nelson's path dependency, this is the distinctive path of a developmental state strategy that began with the urgent motive of national security in the creation of ADD and the Yulgok programme in the 1970s.

LIG's success is the successful realisation of a path-dependent strategy designed, in the name of national security, so that the state absorbs innovation risk while the private sector concentrates on volume production and exports.

4.3 Implications — the future warfare paradigm and structural limits

The future battlefield is shifting toward all-domain mosaic warfare (ADMW) and software-defined weapons systems (SDWS), and with AI-guided loitering munitions and drone swarms deployed in the Russia–Ukraine war, competition to accelerate the OODA loop has begun. But the Statist Triple Helix that worked so well for catch-up innovation is exposing structural limits: weak basic research capability in AI and autonomous systems owing to ADD's monopoly on knowledge; limited rapid prototyping capability and restricted start-up participation; rigid coordination mechanisms centred on DAPA; and one-way technology flow from ADD to firms with universities pushed to the margins.

4.4 Direction of development — the K-DTH 2.0 roadmap

On the basis of SWOT and PEST analysis, the study proposes a roadmap for evolution toward an AI-based K-DTH (Defense Triple Helix) 2.0. In the short term (2025–2028): AI-based battlefield data integration research, a Common Data Framework prototype, and entrenching technology fee and financial support as long-term institutions. In the medium term (2029–2033): reintroducing universities through a defence AI research centre, building standard software and AI architectures, and localising core components. In the long term (from 2034): securing AI-based decision superiority and leading global ADMW standards.

Implications
  • The structure of the success: LIG Nex1's PGM export success is the result of a Statist Triple Helix in which the firm's R&D and business capability is combined with knowledge production and combat verification by ADD and the military, integrated pan-government governance, and the technology fee exemption — an institutional price advantage of more than 20% over European competitors.
  • What the inflection point means: Exports of some KRW 12 trillion to three Middle Eastern countries demonstrate that Korea's defence industry has made a qualitative shift from latecomer to a core actor in the global market.
  • The task of structural reform: Responding to the SDWS and ADMW paradigm shift requires keeping the existing strengths — combat-proven reliability and institutional price advantage — while reintroducing universities into the knowledge space, securing openness in the innovation ecosystem through standard software and AI architectures, and entrenching export finance and technology fee measures as long-term institutions.
  • The ultimate goal: Korea's defence industry should use its PGM export success as a platform to move toward leading global ADMW standards.
