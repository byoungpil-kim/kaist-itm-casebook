Retort-based ambient home meal replacements (HMR) carry a structural limitation: the high-temperature, high-pressure heat treatment needed for commercial sterilization degrades sensory quality — taste, aroma and texture. This case study analyzes how the trade-off between storage stability and sensory quality entrenched across the industry is eased through the reconstruction of product architecture, taking CJ CheilJedang's Bibigo soups and stews as its subject. By processing the liquid and solid components separately and then recombining them, Bibigo redesigned the connections among components and eased the heat load placed on each during sterilization.

Research summary

The analytical framework connects, in sequence, Abernathy and Utterback's (1978) pattern of industrial innovation, Stoneman's (2010) soft innovation and Henderson and Clark's (1990) architectural innovation.
  • Bibigo is found to have eased the heat load on each component during sterilization by processing the liquid and solid parts separately and then recombining them, thereby redesigning the connections among components.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background and need

Korean ready-meal sales grew from about KRW 2.7 trillion in 2015 to about KRW 8.4 trillion in 2024, an average annual growth rate of about 13.8%; as of 2024 HMR products accounted for 9.2% of all food industry sales in Korea, the second-largest single product group after beverages. Among them, retort-based ambient HMR allows long storage and wide distribution without a cold chain, but the high-temperature, high-pressure heat treatment that makes ambient storage possible simultaneously volatilizes flavour compounds, denatures proteins and breaks down texture. The result is a structural trade-off between storage stability and sensory quality.

The essence of the problem lies not in optimizing individual process conditions but in how the relationships among components are set. In a conventional retort process, components of very different physical properties — meat, vegetables, broth — are sealed together in a single container and given the same thermal history. Although each component has a different optimal heat treatment, the process conditions for the whole are set to the most stringent sterilization requirement. Components for which lower heat would suffice are therefore over-processed, degrading texture, losing flavour and breaking down structure.

1.2–1.3 Choice of subject and research questions

The study selects CJ CheilJedang's Bibigo soups and stews as its subject. First, CJ CheilJedang was the leading company by sales in Korea's HMR product group as of 2024. Second, soups and stews are a composite food structure in which solid and liquid parts coexist in one container, so the trade-off appears clearly. Third, innovation attempts such as separate pre-treatment, differentiated process conditions by component and recombination can be traced through registered patents and international filings.

2. Theoretical background — a three-step connection of industry context, innovation goal and innovation means

The study connects three theories in sequence. Abernathy and Utterback's (1978) pattern of industrial innovation frames the industry context: as an industry passes from the fluid phase into the specific phase, the dominant design stabilizes and the focus of innovation shifts to process efficiency and incremental improvement. Sensory quality is then positioned not as an independent innovation goal but as a variable adjusted and managed within given technical constraints. Stoneman's (2010) soft innovation frames the innovation goal: even where a product's physical function does not change greatly, a change in the sensory and aesthetic value consumers perceive counts as innovation. Henderson and Clark's (1990) architectural innovation frames the innovation means: innovation in which the core design of individual components is retained but the way they are connected and the system-level design are reconstructed — and which is especially difficult for incumbents because architectural knowledge becomes tacitly embedded within the organization.

Bibigo's innovation became possible where technical feasibility met permissive regulation. The Korean Food Code specifies sterilization for retort foods as "heat treatment at a product core temperature of 120°C or above for four minutes or longer, or a method of equivalent or greater efficacy," and that clause on equivalent efficacy leaves room for alternative process designs beyond fixed temperature and time conditions.

3. Industry and company environment

3.1 Market structure: changing bases of competition in HMR

Korea's HMR market has evolved through a first generation centered on convenience (instant foods), a second built on chilled and frozen preservation technology (freshness) and a third marked by the spread of meal replacements (taste and variety), into a fourth generation of premium HMR combining technical stability with sensory quality to deliver an experience close to an everyday meal. Bibigo soups and stews are a leading example of that fourth generation, seeking to deliver taste and texture close to home cooking while retaining the convenience of ambient distribution. Domestic sales of soups and stews grew from KRW 301.7 billion in 2020 to KRW 498.5 billion in 2024 — a product group in which the quality trade-off is sharp and market growth is also evident.

3.2–3.4 The dominant process structure and CJ CheilJedang's position

Korea's retort food market took shape with Ottogi's launch of "3-Minute Curry" in 1981, and what functioned as the dominant design was not a particular product but a process structure: sealing the food and then heat-treating it in bulk at high temperature and pressure. Within that structure, companies attempted quality improvement by raising ingredient quality or refining seasoning blends, but so long as the whole product was exposed to the same thermal history during final sterilization the gains were limited. CJ CheilJedang, a comprehensive food company with total revenue of KRW 17.9 trillion in 2024, holds shares of 70% in packaged rice, 64% in canned ham and 45% in dumplings, and has maintained about 39% in soups and stews since 2018.

4. Analysis of Bibigo's innovation mechanizm

4.1 The technical origins of the storage–sensory trade-off

The cause of the trade-off lies less in the particular temperature and time settings than in processing heterogeneous components together within one process structure. Raising heat intensity secures microbiological safety and storage stability but degrades sensory quality through protein denaturation and loss of aroma compounds; lowering it preserves sensory quality but makes it hard to guarantee the microbiological safety ambient distribution requires. The heart of Bibigo's innovation is not the choice of a particular balance point but the redesign of the process structure itself so that damage to perceived taste, aroma and texture is reduced while the same safety conditions are maintained.

The heart of Bibigo's innovation is not simply reducing heat intensity but reconstructing the process architecture to create a structure in which microbiological safety and sensory quality can be achieved together.

4.2 Reconstructing the process architecture

The conventional process applied a single heat treatment with liquid and solid parts combined, and the sterilization conditions for the whole were set around the most heat-sensitive component. The Bibigo process instead separates liquid and solid parts, applies conditions suited to each, and recombines them at the final stage — reconstructing the connections between processes. This is architectural innovation: the core design of the individual components is retained while the way they are connected changes.

Converting the liquid process: the conventional method of mixing and heating extract and powdered seasoning in purified water left high residues of the compounds responsible for retort odour (such as 2-pentylfuran); Bibigo instead heats and extracts from raw ingredients directly, adjusting temperature in stages to strengthen the release of umami compounds. Glutamine rose from 4.3 mg/L under the conventional method to 10.4 mg/L under Bibigo's. Pre-stabilizing the meat solids: under curing conditions of 30% phosphate and 70% starch, mechanical hardness fell by about 63% and drip loss by about 64%; combining this with surface blanching maximized the effect, cutting hardness by about 73% and drip loss by about 76%.

Standardizing the solids: pre-treating solid ingredients such as vegetables and seafood and standardizing them as frozen blocks eased quality variation during filling and changes in physical properties during heat treatment, and made automatic filling and dosing possible. Reducing sterilization heat intensity: microwave heating was applied to raw ingredients for preliminary microbial reduction and combined with retort under relaxed conditions (mild retort). Under the combined microwave and mild retort condition, no total bacteria, heat-resistant bacteria or fungi were detected in chicken, beef or shrimp, satisfying the Food Code's "equivalent or greater efficacy" condition while easing the quality loss caused by excessive heat.

4.3 Implementing soft innovation — quantifying sensory attributes and linking them to process variables

Rather than accepting sensory degradation in the retort process as an unavoidable constraint, Bibigo treated sensory attributes — flavour, texture, aftertaste, appearance — as objects to be measured, compared and adjusted in conjunction with concrete process conditions such as temperature, time, pH and pre-treatment method. After applying an improved process combining pH adjustment with mild heating, evaluations of overall taste, kimchi texture and appearance all rose for the kimchi ingredient, and overall taste, kimchi texture, pork chewiness and appearance all improved in pork kimchi stew. Umami amino acid content, hardness and sensory evaluation results were used as the evidence connecting quantified sensory quality to process conditions.

The significance of Bibigo's soft innovation is that it did not leave sensory value in the realm of qualitative preference but converted it into something manageable within the relationship between process conditions and quality indicators.

4.4 Protecting the innovation — the patent portfolio

Between 2016, after Bibigo's launch, and 2025, CJ CheilJedang filed 487 food-sector patents in Korea and 1,111 including overseas filings, a large quantitative gap over competitors such as Lotte Wellfood and Shinsegae Food. Where competitors' overseas filings are limited, CJ CheilJedang is extending its scope of protection through major jurisdictions and the PCT route. The more process elements such as liquid processing, solid stabilization, frozen blocks and microwave pre-treatment are secured as patent rights, the harder it becomes for later entrants to imitate the same process design or product implementation.

Implications
  • Findings: By processing liquid and solid parts separately and recombining them, Bibigo reconstructed the connections between processes, easing the loss of texture and flavour while meeting commercial sterilization standards. Curing, blanching, frozen blocks and microwave pre-treatment are extensions of existing food processing knowledge, but they are integrated under a new connecting rule — separate processing followed by recombination — making this architectural innovation.
  • Extending process innovation: In the food industry, process innovation needs to extend beyond improving the efficiency of individual processes to redesigning the connections between them. Where optimizing individual processes cannot ease a structural trade-off, architectural reconstruction reviewing the relationships among components and the order of treatment is required.
  • Designing sensory quality: Taste, aroma, texture and appearance should be managed not as qualitative judgements but as quantitative quality targets, designed and verified iteratively by linking process conditions with sensory evaluation data — the foundation for turning soft innovation into a reproducible, extendable system.
  • Combining with intellectual property, and limitations: Process innovation and soft innovation lower the possibility of imitation and lead to sustainable competitive advantage when secured as a patent portfolio. That said, this is a single case study based on secondary sources, with limits in verifying internal decision-making and in generalizability, leaving multiple case studies as a task for future research.
