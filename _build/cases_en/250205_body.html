On 9 April 2021 at Sacheon in South Gyeongsang Province, the first prototype of the KF-21 Boramae rolled out — Korea's first indigenously developed supersonic fighter and the world's eighth. A Korean aerospace industry that had depended on depot maintenance and assembly production of military aircraft until the 1990s is now developing a fighter of its own, some 40 years on. This study analyses KAI's path from licensed production of the KF-16 through co-development of the T-50 to indigenous development of the KF-21, using Cohen and Levinthal's theory of absorptive capacity.

Research summary
  • KAI accumulated manufacturing, design and systems integration capability through licensed production of the KF-16 and co-development of the T-50, and on that foundation succeeded in the indigenous development of the KF-21, a highly demanding fighter. This study analyses that achievement through the lens of absorptive capacity.
  • KAI secured a production base through licensed production and internalised core technologies such as avionics and flight control through co-development, absorbing and reconfiguring external knowledge effectively.
  • As a result, at the KF-21 stage it secured the capability for technological self-reliance, able to develop high-value technologies itself.
  • The study presents KAI's path as "laying the foundation for absorption → strengthening absorptive capacity → extending capability," and draws strategic implications for how a latecomer firm or country can achieve technological self-reliance in a complex product systems (CoPS) industry.

Chapter 1. Introduction

The KF-21 is a 4.5-generation fighter with a top speed of Mach 1.81, a combat radius of 2,900 km and a weapons payload of 7.7 tonnes; after its first successful test flight in July 2022, testing continues toward deployment in 2026. It is a large-scale national strategic programme with a budget above KRW 8 trillion, managed by the Defense Acquisition Program Administration with KAI as prime contractor responsible for systems integration. At the end of 2024, even before full-scale development concluded, a first production contract for 40 Block-I aircraft was signed on the basis of the "provisionally suitable for combat" determination obtained in May 2023. Notably, KAI began full-scale development with no experience of indigenous fighter development at all and succeeded in building a prototype and making its first flight about 11 years after the programme began — a competitive record against the 15 to 20 years or more taken by other developers of major 4.5-generation fighters.

Chapter 2. Theoretical background

2.1–2.3 Weapons acquisition methods and offset trade

The fighter aircraft industry is a classic complex product systems (CoPS) industry, in which many subsystems and components must be integrated interdependently. Before the KF-21, Korea acquired fixed-wing air weapons systems in three ways: direct purchase, as with the introduction of the F-16 in 1986; technology co-production, as with KF-16 licensed production under the 1994 F-16 offset agreement; and full systems R&D led domestically from design through systems integration and test and evaluation, as with the T-50. An offset is a reciprocal cooperation measure agreed when a country imports a weapons system, through which the purchasing country secures industrial and technological benefits such as technology transfer, production participation and component localisation.

2.4 Innovation theory — absorptive capacity

Absorptive capacity is a firm's ability to recognise, acquire, assimilate, internally reconfigure and ultimately exploit knowledge from outside to create innovation. The core characteristics of the concept as set out by Cohen and Levinthal are cumulativeness — learning ability strengthens as experience accumulates — path dependence, whereby past knowledge shapes the absorption of new knowledge, and organisational learning elements such as R&D investment, staff training and organisational structure. Absorptive capacity operates in four stages: acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation.

Chapter 3. Hypothesis

This study asks how KAI achieved technological self-reliance in the progression from licensed production through co-development to indigenous development. The core hypothesis is that the growth of fighter development capability was not the result of a single programme but the product of technology absorption experience and organisational learning accumulated over decades — and further, the result of national policy, an industrial ecosystem, technological cooperation and workforce systems combined. From this, two research questions follow. First, what factors made indigenous fighter development succeed? Second, what does this suggest for the development of advanced domestic industries such as fighter aircraft?

The analysis has three parts. The case analysis reviews the flow of technology accumulation across the KF-16, T-50 and KF-21 programmes; the success factor analysis examines the maturing of development capability through three structures — laying the foundation for absorption, strengthening absorptive capacity, and extending capability. Finally, the performance analysis assesses how that technology accumulation translated into economic results such as jobs and exports and into industrial results such as shorter development times.

Chapter 4. Case analysis

KAI is an aerospace systems integrator established in 1999 through the merger of the aerospace divisions of Samsung Aerospace, Daewoo Heavy Industries and Hyundai Space & Aircraft, and has built and delivered more than 900 aircraft. In its second-quarter 2025 order intake, domestic business alone — including the contract for the remaining KF-21 initial production volume — exceeded KRW 2 trillion, about 65% of total orders of some KRW 3.1 trillion, showing fighter aircraft at the centre of its order book. KAI's timeline divides into a formative period centred on KF-16 licensed production (1980s–2005), a growth period centred on T-50 co-development (2006–2014) and a maturity period centred on KF-21 indigenous development (2015–present).

4.3 The major programmes

The KF-16 programme (pursued 1983–1994) was Korea's first advanced fighter acquisition, securing 120 aircraft in total: 12 complete aircraft through FMS, 36 assembled domestically and 72 built under licence with local content. It gave Korea manufacturing base technologies — aircraft assembly processes, quality assurance systems, test and evaluation capability — but design technology and core software were not transferred. The T-50 programme, begun on the offset principle from KF-16 licensed production, was Korea's first supersonic fighter development; in a structure where KAI handled systems integration, fuselage design and final assembly with Lockheed Martin supporting joint systems development, Korea acquired core process technologies including avionics integration and flight control system development. The KF-21 is the first 4.5-generation fighter Korea has designed independently, with core technologies — avionics and flight control software, a three-dimensional concurrent engineering design system, weapons integration capability — developed domestically.

Chapter 5. Success factors

5.1 Laying the foundation for absorption

Government policy leadership was the starting point. In the political environment of the end of the Cold War, when the United States pressed Korea toward self-reliant defence, the government funded the development costs of a mega-programme requiring trillions of won and guaranteed stable long-term demand, giving firms a basis to reduce risk and concentrate on technological learning. To resolve a structure in which Samsung Aerospace, Daewoo Heavy Industries and Hyundai Space & Aircraft competed and duplicated investment into the late 1990s, the government pursued a support plan for establishing an integrated aerospace corporation, and in 1999 the three companies' aerospace divisions merged to launch KAI. Consolidation concentrated people, facilities and development capability, and created a single channel representing all of Korea in negotiations with foreign firms, greatly strengthening bargaining power.

For technology acquisition, Korea adopted a participatory structure using offsets (KF-16) and a collaborative structure (T-50). On the T-50, KAI formed one-to-one engineer teams with Lockheed Martin (the KAI–LM 1:1 Engineer Team), responding immediately when anomalies arose and absorbing even tacit design knowledge. It established a purchasing office and a chief engineer office in the United States to manage 147 overseas suppliers directly, securing global sourcing capability. Above all, obtaining the flight control operational flight program (Flight Control OFP) — the aircraft's "brain" — through persistent negotiation by both working teams and management was the decisive achievement.

5.2 Strengthening absorptive capacity

At the KF-16 stage, engineers observed production processes at the original manufacturer's site while practising at home, accumulating some 1,600 production technologies including composite machining and electron beam welding. At the T-50 stage, internalising absorbed knowledge began in earnest. Research groups of engineers sharing an interest were activated, and the tracking, sharing and documentation of technical material was made routine; papers and study activity in R&D learning organisations grew from about 20 items in 1994 to more than 70 in 2003 and more than 200 a year recently. The company also introduced TAMS, a technology acquisition management system, allowing technical material to be exchanged in real time among KAI, the government and suppliers and the state of technology acquisition to be tracked. Prior technologies in aerodynamics, flight control and composite structures accumulated by the Agency for Defense Development through KTX-2 exploratory development were transferred to KAI at the full-scale development stage, completing a research institute–industry collaboration model.

5.3 Extending capability

KF-21 development applied COMOK, a three-dimensional concurrent engineering system, integrating verification across design, analysis and production preparation. When a design engineer modifies a model, the structural analysis team immediately reviews strength and safety while the production team simultaneously checks assembly sequence; design error corrections that once took months can now be verified in hours in a digital mock-up environment. In software, the flight control laws that had been supplied as a black box during the T-50 programme — with no way to understand or modify the internal algorithms — were successfully developed independently on the basis of flight test data and simulation results accumulated during T-50 development. The mission computer was also fully localised on the KF-21, and in November 2023 KAI obtained CMMI version 2.2 Level 5, the highest rating, in avionics for aircraft development.

Technological self-reliance is not achieved by simply receiving technology from outside; it comes only when an organisation systematically builds the absorptive capacity to understand, digest and reconfigure it and to create new value from it.

5.4 Results

KF-21 development is a victory for an industrial ecosystem in which more than 700 domestic suppliers participated. Analysis by government and research institutes expects development and production of the KF-21 to generate about KRW 24 trillion in production and some 110,000 jobs. On development time, the KF-21 is expected to take about 15 years from exploratory development to deployment — comparable with the average among advanced aerospace nations (about 13.7 years) and shorter than the Eurofighter (about 16 years) or China's J-10 (about 20 years).

Comparison with similar cases shows what distinguishes Korea's path. In Brazil's AMX (1978–1989), Italy led core design and systems integration while Brazil was confined to production, and the programme did not lead on to indigenous fighter development. Japan's F-2 (1987–2000) secured autonomy in enlarged airframe design and AESA radar but depended on the United States for the engine, flight control software and mission computer, and Japan subsequently turned to international co-development with the United Kingdom and Italy (GCAP) rather than indigenous development. India's Tejas (1983–2015) attempted indigenous development without an adequate technology base, stretching development to 33 years with repeated shortfalls against performance targets. Korea, by contrast, accumulated capability in stages over about 20 years through KF-16 licensed production (11 years) and T-50 co-development (9 years) before beginning indigenous development, minimising development risk and accelerating technological internalisation.

Chapter 6. Conclusion

The success of indigenous KF-21 development is the result of a systematic strategy for strengthening absorptive capacity, sustained over a long period. In laying the foundation for absorption, consolidating the aerospace industry and using offsets created an environment in which external knowledge could be acquired. In strengthening absorptive capacity, the manufacturing and quality management experience of the KF-16 became the base on which the design and systems integration technology of the T-50 could be assimilated more quickly. And in extending capability, the company secured the ability to exploit what it had learned as new technology — a three-dimensional concurrent engineering design environment and independently developed flight control laws among them.

Implications
  • Self-reliance is the product of staged learning: The core is roughly 30 years of staged learning running KF-16 (manufacturing capability) → T-50 (design and systems integration capability) → KF-21 (full self-reliance). India's Tejas failed because indigenous development was attempted without an adequate base; advanced industries such as semiconductors, batteries, hydrogen and aerospace likewise need a staged technological learning path by design.
  • Systematic organisational learning mechanisms: KAI's success came not from a collection of outstanding individuals but from organisational learning infrastructure — R&D learning organisations, the TAMS technical data sharing system, one-to-one engineer teams. When importing technology, an internal learning system must be built alongside it.
  • Designing the structure of technological cooperation: Securing the lead in systems integration, managing 147 overseas suppliers directly, and negotiating to obtain the flight control software all show that contract structure, division of roles and decision rights bear decisively on strengthening absorptive capacity.
  • Government lays the foundation; absorption is the firm's task: Institutions can open opportunities, but the actor that converts technology into competitiveness is ultimately the firm.
  • Internalising core technology: The KF-21 counts as an indigenous fighter not because it is assembled in Korea but because the software and systems integration technologies that constitute its brain — the flight control laws, avionics systems and mission computer — were developed domestically.
