As environmental regulation tightens, the international shipping industry faces ever stricter limits on greenhouse gas emissions from vessels. R&D on eco-friendly ship technologies has intensified in response, and among them the use of LNG as fuel to cut greenhouse gases has drawn attention as a bridge technology until non-fossil fuels are commercialized. This study examines how the product architecture and major systems of a ship changed as eco-friendly ship technologies were introduced, and analyzes the technology development strategy shipbuilders adopted in response, centring on Daewoo Shipbuilding & Marine Engineering's development of core LNG carrier technologies.

1. Introduction: ships and the history of shipbuilding

The development of ships is closely tied to changes in propulsion. From oared vessels through sailing ships that harnessed the wind, shipping arrived at the age of powered vessels; the fuel for those vessels moved from coal to oil, and today the application of LNG — a low-carbon fuel — is expanding as environmental regulation tightens. In the longer term, carbon-free options such as electric propulsion, ammonia and hydrogen are expected to take over. Korea's shipbuilding industry gained its footing with the enactment of the Shipbuilding Industry Promotion Act in 1967; after the "big three" (Daewoo, Hyundai and Samsung) were established in the 1970s, the industry entered high-value-added product markets such as LNG carriers in the mid-1990s, and from the 2000s it has led the world not only in order volume but also in design and construction technology.

2. Background and purpose

2.1 The emergence of eco-friendly ship technology

Following the International Maritime Organization's definition, this study treats as eco-friendly any new-build vessel that meets the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI). The IMO has set a target of cutting carbon dioxide emissions from new-build vessels in stages against the 2008 baseline — 10% from 2015 (EEDI Phase I), 20% from 2020 (Phase II) and 30% from 2025 (Phase III) — reaching a reduction of more than 50% by 2050. This is a level that cannot be met with existing technology and existing modes of vessel operation.

The leading commercialized technology is LNG fuel propulsion. LNG is an eco-friendly fuel that emits 99% less sulphur oxide, 85% less nitrogen oxide and 20% less carbon dioxide than conventional marine fuel oil. Its application continues to widen as a bridge technology until non-fossil fuels are commercialized, and consumption in 2022 was projected to be around five times the 2018 level.

2.3 Theoretical background — architecture theory

Product architecture theory concerns the basic design philosophy governing how the functions required of a product are allocated across its structures (components) and how the interfaces between components are designed. Takahiro Fujimoto, a leading Japanese architecture theorist, proposed a basic classification based on the mapping between product functions and components and on the openness of interfaces (Integral/Modular × Closed/Open), and a deeper classification that extends this to the relationship between a firm's own product architecture and that of its customers.

3. Changes in the product architecture of ships

3.1 Change in the basic architecture classification

A ship has the character of a Closed Integral architecture, in which seven basic functions — buoyancy, propulsion, strength, cargo capacity, stability, motion and maneuvering — map many-to-many onto the eight components (main groups) of the SFI Group Code. For eco-friendly vessels, additional functions are required to cut greenhouse gas emissions through alternative fuels and to raise energy efficiency, and these too must be realized through the combined action of multiple components. Overall integrity therefore increases and the Closed Integral character grows stronger.

3.2 Change in the deeper architecture classification

Seen through the relationship with customers, a ship is not a mass-produced good but is built to order to the customer's requirements — an Integral Outside structure. As eco-friendly ship technologies began to be applied, customer requirements grew more varied: the choice of fuel, measures to raise energy efficiency, the installation of exhaust gas reduction equipment and so on. Shipyards must design and build vessels through far more fine tuning, so the Integral Inside–Integral Outside (IIIO) character has grown stronger.

4. Daewoo Shipbuilding & Marine Engineering case study

4.1 Core technologies of the eco-friendly LNG carrier

An LNG carrier transports natural gas in bulk by cooling it to −163°C, reducing its volume roughly 600-fold. Perfect insulation is impossible, so boil-off gas forms through natural vaporization; left alone it raises pressure inside the cargo tank and can cause an explosion. The core technology therefore lies in minimizing the natural boil-off rate of the cargo tank and either re-injecting the boil-off gas into the tank or using it as fuel. There are four core systems: the LNG cargo containment system (membrane type, licensed exclusively by France's GTT), the gas engine (Germany's MAN ES released a high-pressure engine in 2012 and Finland's Wärtsilä a low-pressure engine in 2015), the LNG fuel gas supply system and the LNG re-liquefaction system. Cargo containment and gas engines are dominated by leading overseas suppliers, whereas the fuel gas supply and re-liquefaction systems are ones Daewoo Shipbuilding concentrated its research on and succeeded in developing and commercializing itself.

4.2 The history of LNG technology development at Daewoo Shipbuilding

Daewoo Shipbuilding recorded its first delivery in 1995, when it handed over Korea's first LNG carrier fitted with a membrane-type cargo containment system. Most vessels at the time used Moss-type tanks, but the company judged that GTT's membrane type, superior in efficiency, would come to dominate the market and adopted it before any other Korean yard. There followed the world's first LNG carrier with onboard regasification (LNG RV) in 2005, the world's first fuel gas supply system for high-pressure gas engines in 2010, a patent licensing agreement supplying its own fuel gas supply system to German engine maker MAN ES in 2013 (a first for any shipyard in the world), the construction of its own cryogenic gas test center in 2014 (again a world first for a shipyard), and the world's first LNG carrier fitted with a high-pressure gas engine, built and delivered in 2016. As of April 2021, Clarksons data put Daewoo Shipbuilding first in the world with 184 LNG carriers delivered cumulatively.

4.3 Technology development strategy — pre-emptive development and composite modularization

Before the world's first marine gas engine appeared in 2012, LNG cargo containment was supplied exclusively by France's GTT, leaving Daewoo Shipbuilding, Hyundai Heavy Industries and Samsung Heavy Industries with little scope to differentiate from one another. At that point Daewoo Shipbuilding concluded that LNG fuel would steadily expand and, noting that MAN ES — the leading marine engine maker — was developing a gas engine, set and executed a strategy of pre-emptively developing systems that would connect to it.

The LNG fuel gas supply system (Case I) differs greatly from earlier systems built on general-purpose equipment in that the fuel pressurization process, previously carried out inside the engine, is instead carried out in the fuel supply system. Noting that MAN ES was struggling to implement pressurization within the engine, Daewoo Shipbuilding concentrated on developing a separate system capable of supplying high-pressure gas to the engine and succeeded in doing so first in the world. It went on to sign a licensing agreement with MAN ES, which wanted to supply the engine and fuel supply system as a package. Adding the pressurization process raised internal integrity; the core equipment — the cryogenic high-pressure pump and the high-pressure vaporizer — was developed jointly with leading overseas suppliers, and most components were subsequently localized.

The LNG re-liquefaction system (Case II) liquefies boil-off gas and re-injects it into the cargo tank. Previously this relied on inefficient methods adapted from onshore systems. Daewoo Shipbuilding noticed that the LNG fuel gas supply system it had developed alongside the gas engine already had high-capacity compression, and used it to develop a re-liquefaction system on an entirely new concept. This sharply reduced the electric power consumed in the process, delivering a dramatic energy saving, and removing the separate compressor also greatly improved cost and space efficiency. The economic benefit from fuel savings and cargo preservation reaches roughly USD 5 million per year for a single large LNG carrier.

As marine fuel shifted to LNG, Daewoo Shipbuilding identified the core technology areas it could develop itself and that could generate market demand, then developed and integrated the corresponding systems ahead of the market. In a market of rising product integrity, it took competitive advantage by responding through composite modularization.

This concentration also shows in the patent data. Filings on related patents rose from 2007, when the company began developing the fuel gas supply system, and again from 2012, when it began developing the re-liquefaction system; R&D spending likewise rose steadily over the same period.

4.4 Responding to rising integrity

In 2014 Daewoo Shipbuilding became the first shipyard in the world to build its own cryogenic gas test center, allowing it to test the performance and durability of prototypes in-house when developing LNG and other cryogenic gas systems. This made development schedules more flexible and made it easier to identify and resolve in advance the problems that would arise once systems were installed on an actual vessel. (Hyundai Heavy Industries built comparable facilities only in 2017, Samsung Heavy Industries in 2021.) On the customer side, the company runs a "Voice of Customer" exercise every six months during the warranty period of delivered vessels, gathering feedback not only from customer head-office staff but also from the crews who actually operate the ships. Since 2012 it has also held an "LNGC User Forum" during GASTECH, the world's largest gas industry exhibition, bringing together customers' senior engineers and top management to discuss long-term direction.

4.5 Results

Results came in earnest once the composite modularization of the fuel supply and re-liquefaction systems was complete. In 2014 the company won orders for 37 vessels — roughly half of all LNG carriers ordered worldwide — and from 2017 its delivery record ran far ahead of competitors. The fuel gas supply system won the IR52 Jang Young Shil Award in 2013 and the Korea Technology Grand Prize and Top 10 Mechanical Technology Award in 2014; the re-liquefaction system won the IR52 Jang Young Shil Award in 2015 (best of the year), reinforcing the company's image as a leader in LNG technology. With Korea's three shipbuilders sustaining their technological lead, Korea maintains a wide gap over competitors China and Japan in the global LNG carrier market.

5. Conclusion

As eco-friendly ship technologies were introduced to meet environmental regulation, the functions demanded of a vessel grew more varied and product integrity increased. Building on experience and know-how accumulated over many years with LNG carriers, Daewoo Shipbuilding developed the world's first LNG fuel gas supply system and then a new re-liquefaction system integrated with it — a composite modularization strategy that secured its competitive advantage in the market. Korea's shipbuilding industry has long stood at the top of the world, but its scope had been confined to designing and building complete vessels while core equipment was sourced from leading overseas suppliers. By achieving self-sufficiency in core technology, Daewoo Shipbuilding extended its role beyond supplying finished vessels to supplying core systems as well.

Implications
  • Composite modularization works: In a market where product integrity is rising, pre-emptively developing and integrating the core technology areas a firm can build itself and that can generate market demand became the source of competitive advantage.
  • From building finished products to supplying core systems: Developing the core systems of LNG carriers in-house and applying them across many vessels extended the business from supplying finished ships to supplying core systems.
  • What remains: So far core systems have been supplied only for the company's own vessels or provided externally under license. With maritime environmental regulation extending to vessels already in service and the retrofit market expected to grow, the company needs to build a capability to sell systems as standalone products and to evolve the architecture of its core systems from Integral Inside–Integral Outside toward Integral Inside–Modular Outside.
