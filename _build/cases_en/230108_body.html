Korea invests in R&D at one of the highest ratios to GDP in the world, yet suffers an "R&D paradox" in which only about 1% of that research is actually used in commercialisation. Universities likewise spend around KRW 10 trillion a year on research but earn only about KRW 100 billion from technology transfer — again roughly 1%. This case study compares the current state and performance of the university technology holding companies established since the scheme began in 2008, examines two strong jointly established cases — Mirae Science & Technology Holdings and Busan Regional Universities Technology Holdings — and analyses overseas cases including the UK's UCSF and Germany's EXIST and HTGF, to draw out ways of invigorating university technology holding companies.

Research summary
  • This study looks for ways to invigorate technology holding companies, and for wider implications, through cases of high-performing university technology holding companies and strong overseas cases of support for university commercialisation.
  • For the business model of a technology holding company, which generates returns by taking equity in technology-based firms, what matters is that its portfolio companies cross the chasm, grow and achieve a value-up.
  • This requires quantitative support: more specialist staff, regulatory improvement, and secure capital and funds.
  • It also requires qualitative effort, such as running startup programmes and incubation centres and expanding networks of accelerators and venture capital firms for co-investment and follow-on investment.
  • Through these case analyses, the study hopes that discussion and research will continue on how to develop university technology holding companies as a major incubation and investment resource in the technology commercialisation ecosystem.

1. Introduction — a startup ecosystem concentrated in the capital region, and the R&D paradox

The government has supported many policies to invigorate the venture and startup ecosystem over recent years, but that ecosystem has itself become concentrated in the capital region. The number of technology startups differs by more than 60% between the capital region and the rest of the country, and the number of venture capital firms available to support their growth differs more than tenfold. Not a single unicorn valued at KRW 1 trillion exists outside the capital region.

Against this backdrop, university technology holding companies are the vehicle through which universities themselves can become entrepreneurial universities. They not only generate income contributing to university finances but can also be the best available alternative for supporting university startups that private venture capital avoids on grounds of investment risk and profitability (Kim Do-sung and Ahn Sung-pil, 2020). Since the technology holding company scheme took effect in Korea in 2008, 78 such companies were in operation as of June 2021. Most of the active ones, however, are capital-region holding companies backed by leading universities, while in the regions it is jointly funded holding companies established by several universities together that are thriving.

The single-university model secures maximum autonomy for the university and makes it easy to reflect key stakeholders' views in important decisions in the university's interest, but it makes technology-linked commercialisation collaboration difficult and makes it hard to secure specialist staff such as accountants, patent attorneys and investment professionals (Park Min-kyu, 2015). The jointly established model, by contrast, brings in external institutions or companies beyond the university and solves the problems of limited resources and specialist staff by pooling funding that no single university could provide (STEPI, 2014).

2. Theoretical background — the J-curve, the chasm, accelerators and signalling theory

The study uses Howard Love's startup J-curve model (six stages: create, release, morph, model, scale, harvest) and Moore's chasm theory as its analytical framework. Moore (1991) held that in the process of innovation adoption a "chasm" arises from differences in values between adopter categories, appearing most sharply in the gap between early adopters and the early majority. How to bridge this break in expanding from the early market to the mainstream market is critically important for technology-based firms.

The accelerator is a relatively new phenomenon, with the US's Y Combinator in 2005 generally seen as the first: an organisation that helps the startups it selects overcome early-stage obstacles and establish themselves as independent businesses. Its business structure comprises funding structure, strategic focus, selection process, incubation programme and network (Miller and Bound, 2011). In technology transfer, among the legal, administrative and marketing models, the marketing model — which actively applies marketing principles such as STP — is the most proactive form, and research suggests that a demand-pull approach identifying potential adopting firms is more effective than a supply-push approach promoting the technologies on hand (Hwang Hyun-deok and Chung Sun-yang, 2015). Finally, from a signalling perspective, in startup investment where information is scarce venture capital firms form investment networks and co-invest to reduce information asymmetry, and VCs with larger networks show better exit performance (Abell et al., 2007).

3. Comparing domestic and overseas cases

3.1 Current state and performance of Korean technology holding companies

Grouping technology holding companies by capital size and averaging performance over three years (2019–2021), those with capital of KRW 6 billion or more performed best on every measure: dedicated staff (9.1), subsidiaries (36.2), equity invested, and recoveries and returns. By type of establishment, the comparison covers 61 single-university holding companies, 10 joint holding companies (involving 59 universities) and one holding company in the form of a new-technology startup specialist firm (such as Mirae Science & Technology Holdings).

Because the sample is small and variables are not controlled, this analysis cannot claim statistical significance, but three possibilities can be inferred. First, the performance of a technology holding company was unrelated to the number of founders, patent applications and registrations, or technology transfers at the contributing university; capital, staffing and the size of funds raised had more effect. Second, joint holding companies performed better on average than single-university ones. Third, joint holding companies made cash investments in firms more actively than single-university ones, showing that joint holding companies — mostly located in the regions — are serving as regional startup investment institutions.

3.2.1 Strong joint case ① Mirae Science & Technology Holdings

In October 2013 four science and technology specialist universities — KAIST, GIST, DGIST and UNIST — signed an agreement to establish a joint technology holding company, incorporated on 17 March 2014. Between 2014 and early 2019 Mirae Science & Technology Holdings invested KRW 7.6 billion in 44 companies from its own account; on the strength of their strong growth it raised its first fund through the education account of the Korea Fund of Funds in December 2018, and by the end of 2022 had raised five funds totalling KRW 44.75 billion and invested KRW 28.95 billion in 95 companies. As a TIPS operator it also linked 34 subsidiaries to TIPS matching.

Among its strongest investments, Clinomics — UNIST's first faculty startup, in which it invested KRW 200 million in October 2015 — listed on KOSDAQ on 4 December 2020, returning about 35 times the original investment. Standard Energy, which received early investment and TIPS support, developed secondary batteries for large-scale energy storage systems and attracted KRW 65 billion in follow-on investment from Lotte Chemical.

From 2018 the "public research diffusion and commercialisation programme," run jointly with Korea Science and Technology Holdings, selected more than 50 promising startups a year and supported the full technology startup cycle from mentoring and technology transfer matching to investment and follow-on support. Through this programme Mirae Science & Technology Holdings invested about KRW 8 billion in 28 companies, which went on to secure about KRW 60 billion in follow-on investment and KRW 36 billion in R&D funding. On the network side, around 30 subsidiaries co-invested some KRW 14 billion with about 30 investment institutions, and more than KRW 400 billion in follow-on investment was linked through some 200 institutions (counting duplicates).

In a startup investment ecosystem marked by high information asymmetry, co-investment served as a form of third-party validation that reduced the imbalance, and network and collaboration sent implicit signals that built a cycle of growth.

3.2.2 Strong joint case ② Busan Regional Universities Technology Holdings

Busan Regional Universities Technology Holdings was established in September 2015 as a core project of "TNT (Talent and Technology) 2030," an initiative to make Busan a top-30 global innovation city by 2030. Sixteen universities in the Busan area and Busan Technopark participate as shareholders, with a contribution from the Busan Metropolitan Government. Its capital now stands at KRW 16.2 billion. Beginning with the first Busan joint personal investment association in 2016, it has raised five funds and invested in 95 companies, including about KRW 10 billion in 49 companies over the four years 2019–2022. As a TIPS operator it linked investment and TIPS support to 17 companies, and it runs "FLY for Start-up," providing mentoring, networks, global capability and investment to startups in the Busan–Ulsan–Gyeongnam region, and "FLY for Scale-up," supporting scale-up investments of KRW 1 billion or more.

Busan Regional Universities Technology Holdings' record of investing in subsidiaries (95) exceeds the combined total of every other technology holding company in the region. Even compared with Pusan National University Technology Holdings — among the best capitalised of the single-university companies, with KRW 7.9 billion in capital and 24 subsidiaries — the jointly established Busan company has achieved stronger results in a shorter period.

3.3 Overseas university technology commercialisation — the UK's UCSF and Germany's EXIST and HTGF

Since 1985 UK universities and research institutes have established and run dedicated technology transfer organisations, which developed into integrated technology commercialisation support companies handling not only technology transfer and consulting but also incubation and the founding of and investment in companies. The single greatest influence on that development was the University Challenge Seed Fund (UCSF), approved in 1999. Through UCSF, 15 investment partnerships were formed across 31 universities and six research institutes, with five more created in 2001. Participating universities had to provide 25% of each fund themselves; the money could be used only to invest in firms commercialising university-held technology, and investment in any one firm was capped at GBP 250,000 (about KRW 450 million). Government support has since ended, but universities continue to run UCSF with their own funds — a case of a scheme that began with government support and passed naturally to the universities.

Germany runs EXIST, a startup support programme through industry, academia and research launched in 1997, along three lines of support — fostering incubation centres at universities and research institutes, funding new firms, and encouraging advanced technology startups — and currently supports 142 universities. Alongside it, the government–private joint fund HTGF (High-Tech Gründerfonds) provides follow-on support by investing about EUR 500,000 (roughly KRW 670 million) in seed-stage startups that conventional VCs tend to avoid, taking a 15% equity stake. Since 2005 HTGF has invested in 680 startups, raising more than EUR 4 billion from outside investors, arranging more than 1,900 follow-on financings and achieving more than 160 exits, making it Germany's largest venture investment fund. Because HTGF's purpose is not returns but identifying innovative startups and drawing in follow-on investment, it resembles Korea Venture Investment Corporation's fund of funds — with the difference that a government institution operates the investment capital directly.

4. Conclusion

Korean technology holding companies performed better the more capital and fund commitments and the more staff they had. Invigorating them requires securing staff, expanding capital to support equity investment, expanding dedicated fund support, and improving rules such as the mandatory equity stake in subsidiaries. The study has limits, however: it compared companies without measuring qualitative aspects such as the quality of the technology held by the associated universities or the entrepreneurship of the founders, and it could not control the many variables of a complex startup and investment ecosystem, so its inferences carry no statistical significance.

Implications
  • Quantitative support: Since capital, fund size and specialist staff all showed a positive relationship with performance, the priority is regulatory improvement — securing staff, expanding capital, supporting dedicated funds and easing mandatory equity-holding provisions.
  • Qualitative effort: Expanding support for accelerator programmes, public research diffusion projects and university technology management (TMC) programmes should help technology-based startups cross the chasm and achieve a value-up.
  • What the overseas cases suggest: The UK's integrated technology commercialisation support companies show that linking or merging technology holding companies with incubation centres is one option, while Germany's HTGF offers a model in which the government operates investment capital directly with the aim of stimulating startups rather than earning returns, thereby closing the funding gap.
  • An ecosystem view: To develop university technology holding companies as a major incubation and investment resource in the technology commercialisation ecosystem, central and local government must secure resources and improve regulation, while networks among universities and across the investment ecosystem must keep expanding.
