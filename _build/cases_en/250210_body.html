As the blood donor population falls sharply, the blood supply crisis is deepening. Platelets are the most urgent case, with a shelf life of only five to seven days. DewCell, a Korean bio venture founded in 2021, is a latecomer — yet within four years it achieved 2.7 times the production yield of its competitor and is attempting the world's first 50-litre-scale culture of artificial platelets. This study analyses the factors behind that success through the core competency, dynamic capabilities and triple helix frameworks.

Research summary
  • As the limits of the donation-based system and the global blood supply crisis deepen, artificial blood technology is drawing attention as the next-generation substitute, and platelets in particular — with their short shelf life and chronic shortage — make developing artificial production technology especially urgent.
  • The study analyses, from a management-of-technology perspective, how DewCell, a Korean bio venture founded in 2021, secured a leading position in artificial platelets in a short time despite being a latecomer.
  • It examines the market and technological characteristics of the artificial platelet industry and analyses DewCell's growth through the theoretical frameworks of core competency, dynamic capabilities and the triple helix.
  • DewCell is found to have achieved post-catch-up growth, solving the industry's bottleneck and creating a new path.
  • This points to an effective strategic direction through which latecomers in bio deep tech can overcome their limits.

Chapter 1. Introduction

1.1 Background

In Korea, actual supply in 2024 reached only 5,407 units against a daily requirement of 5,482 (91.4%), leaving less than a day's stock. Platelet products in particular hold only 1.8 days of stock against five days for red cell products, keeping them permanently at caution or alert level. The root cause is the sharp fall in blood donors: donors in their teens fell 47.5%, from about 1.5 million in 2015 to about 780,000 in 2024. Directed donation to secure scarce platelets rose 7.3-fold between 2018 and 2021, and an illegal market in blood sales has emerged at around KRW 2 million a pack. In response, artificial platelet technology — differentiating induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into megakaryocytes to mass-produce platelets — is emerging as the next-generation alternative.

1.2 Research topic and questions

The artificial platelet industry still faces the decisive difficulty of economically viable mass production (scale-up). Japan's Megakaryon, the global first mover founded in 2011, raised more than KRW 100 billion and in 2019 became the first in the world to enter phase I trials with artificial platelets — but its process, built around specialised bioreactors, ran into efficiency losses at scale-up and commercialisation difficulties, forcing a process redesign. This shows that the bottleneck in artificial platelet development at present is not basic scientific knowledge but innovation in the process and the design of the business model. DewCell, by contrast, achieved 2.7 times the production yield and cut the process to a third of the time, diversifying a single platform technology across three markets and targeting early revenue from 2026 — an unusual case.

Chapter 2. Theoretical background

2.1 The artificial platelet industry

Artificial platelet production runs in three stages: upstream, differentiating iPSCs into megakaryocytes; midstream, culturing them at scale in a bioreactor to release platelets; and downstream, separating, purifying and quality-controlling the platelets from the culture medium. The core difficulty is economically viable mass production: the larger the bioreactor, the harder it is to keep the internal environment uniform, excessive agitation physically damages megakaryocytes, and cytokines costing millions of won per gram account for a large share of cost. In addition, the immortalisation strategy adopted by some first movers — introducing genes associated with carcinogenesis — increases the burden of proving long-term safety, so a non-engineered approach minimising genetic manipulation is more favourable for regulatory approval. In value chain terms, the core value added concentrates in manufacturing, and a firm's core competitiveness lies in process technology.

2.2 Innovation theory framework

The study uses three theoretical lenses. Core competency (Prahalad and Hamel, 1990) is the collective learning of an organisation meeting three conditions: customer value, differentiation from competitors and extendability. Dynamic capabilities (Teece et al., 1997) is the ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences in response to rapidly changing environments, operating in three stages: sensing → seizing → transforming. The triple helix (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 1995, 2000) holds that innovation is created through interaction among university, industry and government, and matters especially in fields such as bio and healthcare where close collaboration among industry, academia, research institutes, hospitals and government is essential.

Chapter 3. Research approach

This is a single case study examining how DewCell, a latecomer in the artificial platelet industry, overtook the first mover to establish a leading position. DewCell was judged to meet both of the conditions Yin (2014) sets out as making a single case study appropriate: an extreme or unique case, and a revelatory case. The analytical framework applies the three theoretical lenses together. Core competency explains the technological advantage delivered by a proprietary production platform; dynamic capabilities explains the process of turning a single platform into a diversified business portfolio; and the triple helix explains ecosystem building through collaboration across industry, academia, research institutes, hospitals and government.

The research material was gathered mainly from secondary sources — IR materials, government programme announcements, Ministry of Food and Drug Safety notices, patents and press coverage — and triangulated, then analysed using pattern matching and explanation building. Validity was secured through the four criteria of construct, internal and external validity and reliability.

Chapter 4. The DewCell case

4.1 Company overview and history

DewCell, founded in October 2021, is Korea's first and only artificial platelet bio venture, with 24 employees as of November 2025. It differs from the typical bio start-up in that it was founded not by R&D-centred researchers but by industry professionals experienced in commercialisation and quality management. Beginning with TIPS selection in March 2022, it has secured more than KRW 10 billion in cumulative government R&D funding, and in July 2023 in particular it became the lead institution for the "cell-based artificial blood manufacturing and demonstration platform development programme" (2023–2029, KRW 42.5 billion in total) — the hub of a consortium spanning some 20 institutions including Pusan National University, Samsung Medical Center and GC Cell.

4.2 Core competency: internalising process technology

DewCell's business rests on the en-aPLT™ (engineered artificial platelet) platform, composed of three strategic innovations. First, the MKp-MCB (megakaryocyte progenitor master cell bank) strategy. Unlike the conventional approach of starting differentiation from iPSCs every time, DewCell differentiates iPSCs to the megakaryocyte progenitor stage and freezes them, thawing them for production when needed, cutting the total process from 26 days to nine. This raises production turnover 2.9-fold and cuts manufacturing cost by 40–50%. Second, a non-engineered safety strategy minimising genetic manipulation; in August 2025 the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety classified cell-based artificial blood as an advanced biological product, securing a regulatory fast track through conditional approval. Third, using an industry-standard general-purpose bioreactor (the Sartorius Biostat STR) while systematically optimising process variables through design of experiments (DoE). Raising expression of the biomarker CD42b from an initial 40% to more than 90% was the key achievement.

The process innovation produced quantitative results. In a 3-litre bioreactor the company achieved a yield of 2×10¹⁰ platelets per litre, about 2.7 times its competitor's, with a process about 2.9 times faster. Having acquired a GMP-grade production facility, DewCell is attempting the world's first artificial platelet culture at 50-litre scale, about 17 times the 3-litre level.

DewCell's process technology meets all three of Prahalad and Hamel's conditions for a core competency. A nine-day process and 2.7 times the yield cut the final product price by 40–50%, delivering customer value. The integrated system in which MKp-MCB, DoE optimisation and two-step purification are organically combined is hard to imitate even though the individual elements are disclosed in patents and papers, because the know-how of integrating and coordinating them is accumulated within the organisation as tacit knowledge (differentiation from competitors). And artificial platelets produced by that same process technology extend across three markets (extendability). DewCell's core competency lies not in a product but in a process, and because it uses a general-purpose bioreactor rather than becoming locked to specific equipment, it also avoids the trap of core rigidity that Leonard-Barton warned of.

What matters is that DewCell's core competency lies not in a product but in a process. Competitors can imitate the final products — the branches and leaves — but imitating the roots, the process technology, is extremely difficult.

4.3 Dynamic capabilities: leveraging the platform

On the foundation of the en-aPLT™ platform, DewCell built a three-track portfolio securing short-, medium- and long-term revenue simultaneously, overcoming the death valley typical of bio start-ups. Track 1, DCB-301 (the culture medium additive i-ahPLys™), is a lysate extracting growth factors from disrupted artificial platelets; as a research-use-only (RUO) grade product requiring no clinical trials it can be sold immediately, providing early revenue. Track 2, DCB-103 (the osteoarthritis therapeutic i-aPLP™), is a standardised product solving the quality variability of PRP; in an osteoarthritis animal model, the high-dose group's Mankin score for cartilage damage halved from 8 to 4, and the results were presented at OARSI, the world's largest osteoarthritis congress, in April 2025. Track 3, DCB-101 (for transfusion), is the long-term product intended to replace donated platelets entirely.

This three-track strategy implements Teece's sensing (identifying three market opportunities at once) → seizing (allocating government R&D strategically across the tracks) → transforming (a real-options effect in which the three tracks reinforce one another). The tracks differ in market entry timing (2026/2028/2033), regulatory barrier (none/medium/high) and market size (small/medium/large), forming a portfolio of low correlation — an application of Markowitz's modern portfolio theory. One investment industry figure, at the time of the 2023 investment, called the three-track strategy "a wise choice that avoids the all-or-nothing risk typical of bio start-ups."

4.4 Triple helix: building an artificial blood ecosystem

At the heart of DewCell's strategy is that it became not a rule taker following regulation but a rule maker designing it alongside the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Because artificial platelets are a world-first product with no existing guidelines, DewCell — as lead institution of the artificial blood programme — held regular meetings with ministry officials to work out quality standards, safety test design and clinical trial design together, culminating in the classification of cell-based artificial blood as an advanced biological product in August 2025. With universities and research institutes it formed a structure of joint knowledge creation, receiving iPSC core technology transferred from Pusan National University and conducting joint bioreactor scale-up research with the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB). In industry it pursued horizontal collaboration rather than vertical integration, working with Sartorius, the world's leading bioreactor maker, and PL BioScience, Europe's leading human platelet lysate firm (a partnership worth USD 2 million); and with hospitals it built a clinical network with Samsung Medical Center.

Theoretically, DewCell's case corresponds to Triple Helix III — the balanced, industry-led form in which three actors collaborate strategically with the firm at the hub. From DiMaggio's perspective of institutional entrepreneurship, DewCell became a participant designing the rules themselves, creating a regulatory first-mover advantage. Its industrial partnerships also show a strategic judgement consistent with Teece's profiting from innovation: allying externally for the bioreactor (a generic complementary asset) while internalising process optimisation know-how (a specialised complementary asset). As a result DewCell secured the highest network centrality in the artificial blood ecosystem, becoming its architect.

Chapter 5. Conclusion

DewCell's success was a process of post-catch-up going beyond simple imitation, the result of technological advantage (process innovation), strategic flexibility (portfolio diversification) and structural advantage (ecosystem leadership) reinforcing one another in synergy. Learning systematically from the first mover's failures — dependence on specialised equipment leading to scale-up failure, and a single product leading to the death valley — without repeating them, DewCell created a new path in process internalisation on general-purpose equipment and a three-track portfolio, achieving results equal to or better than its competitor's on a third of the capital.

Implications
  • Path creation: Rather than imitating technology, a firm should look for its own alternative path solving the industry's core bottlenecks — scale-up, cost and regulation — to secure a decisive technological lead.
  • A portfolio built on platform thinking: Rather than a single product, a firm should productise the by-products and intermediate outputs of its core technology so that cash flows before the final goal is reached, and must include a track capable of early revenue (requiring no clinical trials) to overcome the death valley.
  • Rule maker status as an institutional entrepreneur: Participating as lead institution in a government R&D programme allows direct consultation with regulators, and becoming the hub of an industry–academia–research–hospital–government network allows a firm to shape the industry's structure in its own favour.
  • A three-in-one strategy: DewCell's growth formula is the combination of breaking the industry's core bottleneck, diversifying markets and pre-empting rule-maker status in the ecosystem — pursuing technological, business and ecosystem innovation together rather than concentrating on any one. This suggests that for a latecomer to overtake, resetting direction matters more than speed.
