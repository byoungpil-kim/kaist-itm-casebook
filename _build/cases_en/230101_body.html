Registered in 2006 as the first research institute company in the Daedeok Innopolis, Kolmar BNH licensed HEMOHIM technology from the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute and successfully commercialised it as an immune-function health functional food. Through overseas market development and market expansion it has since recorded 12% CAGR growth even after its 2015 listing. This case study analyses how the company succeeded in commercialising the contributed technology and entering the market, and what strategy it then used to enter other markets, through the lens of entry strategy and expansion strategy.

Research summary
  • Registered in 2006 as the first research institute company in the Daedeok Innopolis, Kolmar BNH licensed HEMOHIM technology from the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute and successfully commercialised it as an immune-function health functional food; through overseas market development and market expansion it has recorded 12% CAGR growth even after its 2015 listing.
  • In the early commercialisation stage Kolmar BNH chose and entered its beachhead market well, then grew into a leading player in health functional food OEM/ODM through expansion strategies including a merger, overseas entry and stronger capabilities in ingredients.
  • The case suggests that early-stage firms such as research institute companies should build an entry strategy that clearly targets an accessible beachhead market on the basis of their core capabilities, and then expand into connected adjacent markets.

1. Background and purpose

Research institute companies, created to commercialise public research results directly, have grown from the first — Kolmar BNH in 2006 — to more than 1,569 established cumulatively (as of December 2022), generating economic results of KRW 1,126.8 billion in total revenue and 6,007 jobs as of 2021. Since the system was established, five such companies have listed (Kolmar BNH, Sugentech, Syntekabio, GenSystem and Mindslab), but only Kolmar BNH has continued to grow after listing.

Existing literature has attributed Kolmar BNH's success to the ability and passion of the researchers, the experience of the partner firm and policy support from the research institute (Choi Jong-in, 2012), to technology, market and business-actor capability factors (Ham Hyung-wook, 2015), and to resource-based factors and networking (Lee Jong-hwan, 2019). No study, however, has explained the process by which the company diversified its products and customer channels and grew after the initial success of commercialising HEMOHIM — the subject of this case study.

2. Theoretical background

2.1 Research institute companies

The research institute company is a scheme created to promote the direct commercialisation of public technology. A company meeting the requirements — establishment within a special R&D zone, at least a 10% equity stake held by the founding institution and so on — is registered with the approval of the Minister of Science and ICT under Article 9 of the Special Act on the Promotion of Special R&D Zones. The aim is to combine the technological capability of research institutes with private capital and management to create a virtuous ecosystem of R&D → commercialisation → reinvestment. Three types exist: joint-venture, existing-firm-conversion and new-startup. Because these companies begin with technology from a public research institute, they are characterised by a strong technology source.

2.2 Beachhead markets and adjacent markets

In Bill Aulet's 24-step framework in Disciplined Entrepreneurship (2013), a beachhead market is a relatively small but highly targeted market segment: a firm enters early, raises its share, and on that base gains the opportunity to expand into larger markets. Entering a beachhead market requires understanding and segmenting the market, developing a value proposition, delivering a single consistent message, and distributing the product or service effectively. Success in the beachhead market then supports expansion into adjacent markets, with the value proposition and distribution strategy recalibrated to those customers' needs.

2.3 Core competency

Hamel and Prahalad (1990) defined core competency as the essential capability a firm must develop and maintain to secure competitive advantage. A core competency must create customer value, be difficult for competitors to replicate, and be applicable across a range of product lines or market entries.

3. The market: health functional foods

Health functional foods are made using ingredients or components with functionality beneficial to the human body; they can be manufactured and sold only after functional and safety evaluation, including animal and human trials, and recognition by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. The Korean market was worth about KRW 5 trillion in 2021 and an estimated KRW 6,142.9 billion in 2022, growing at close to 20% in almost every one of the past three years under the influence of COVID-19. The global market was worth about KRW 230 trillion in 2022.

The heart of the business is functional ingredients. Unlike listed ingredients such as red ginseng, probiotics and vitamins, over which no exclusive rights exist, individually recognised ingredients are those whose function the Minister of Food and Drug Safety has recognised individually; only the firm concerned may manufacture and sell them, and margins are far higher. Individually recognised health functional foods rose from about 11% of sales in 2017 to about 21% in 2021. Developing such an ingredient, however, takes an average of KRW 1 billion and about seven years.

4. The company

Kolmar BNH traces its origins to Sunbiotech, a joint-venture research institute company founded in February 2004 by Kolmar Korea (about KRW 620 million in cash) and the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute (technology valued at about KRW 380 million). It was designated the first Daedeok Innopolis research institute company in 2006, merged with Korea Foodipharm in 2012 and changed its name to Kolmar BNH, and listed on KOSDAQ through a SPAC merger in 2015. Consolidated revenue in 2022 was KRW 575.9 billion.

Its flagship product HEMOHIM is a herbal composite developed over some eight years by a team led by Dr Cho Sung-kee at the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute, produced by mixing equal weights of Angelica gigas, Cnidium officinale and white peony root and extracting them in hot water. It was the first new substance developed with indigenous Korean technology to be certified as a health functional food, and its efficacy in improving immune function was demonstrated, earning recognition as an individually recognised ingredient from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. HEMOHIM is a mega-hit product accounting for about 20% of Kolmar BNH's revenue today. On the strength of this ingredient pipeline and its formulation development and production capability, the company sells B2B to some 300 firms at home and abroad — including Atomy, Yuhan, E-mart and Chong Kun Dang — and has annual production capacity of about KRW 500 billion across its Eumseong and Sejong plants.

5. Innovation case analysis

5.1 Initial market entry strategy

Winning regulatory recognition for HEMOHIM was not straightforward. A first application for health functional food ingredient recognition in 2004 was rejected over ingredient content and standardisation, and only after accumulating know-how in animal efficacy testing and in designing clinical efficacy trials on sub-healthy subjects — through a national research project with the Atomic Energy Research Institute — did the company obtain recognition from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety in August 2006. Once HEMOHIM, previously sold only as a tea product, became the first product recognised for improving immune function, revenue rose nearly fourfold in 2007.

Revenue growth then stalled, until the company found a marketing partner in 2009 in the multi-level distributor Atomy and grew rapidly. To build member confidence, Atomy took members on tours of the Kolmar BNH plant where HEMOHIM is produced and held one-day seminars at its headquarters, continually highlighting the manufacturer's technology and production capability as it grew its membership.

In its early days HEMOHIM cost KRW 770,000 for 60 sachets — a price point with very poor accessibility — because of its high unit production cost. But once Atomy generated steady demand, the company raised plant utilisation and increased the size of its extraction tanks to achieve economies of scale. Building an automated line covering ingredient extraction and concentration, pouch filling, sterilisation, drying and packaging, it cut the price to KRW 148,000 for 30 sachets, and later to KRW 76,500 per set — close to a tenth of the original price — allowing it to compete even with red ginseng products, the archetypal immune-boosting item.

Kolmar BNH set its beachhead market well within the health functional food market — the segment for improving immunity — delivered a consistent message to that market (low price, high quality), and secured an effective channel and partner in Atomy, thereby establishing itself in the beachhead market.

5.2 Expansion into adjacent markets

Having established itself in the beachhead market, Kolmar BNH weighed the potential and priority of adjacent markets, targeting the global immunity market first, then the domestic health functional food market, and finally the global health functional food market.

Overseas entry. Beginning with the United States in 2010, the company now exports to 25 countries including Japan, Canada, Taiwan, Singapore, Russia, Australia and China. Starting with the USD 5 million Export Tower award in 2013, it reached USD 100 million in 2020 and USD 200 million in 2021, and exports now account for more than 30% of revenue. In the United States, HEMOHIM had already demonstrated safety through FDA testing and completed patent registration in 2004 while still in development; entry succeeded through Atomy's local subsidiary and a "affordable premium" strategy of low price and high quality suited to the home of network marketing. China, by contrast, was entered through localisation — establishing Jiangsu Kolmar, an ODM production base with KRW 200 billion of capacity, and Yantai Kolmar, a joint venture with Atomy — and as of 2022 China accounts for about 50% of exports by destination.

The Korea Foodipharm merger. Having succeeded in commercialising HEMOHIM but still drawing a large share of revenue from cosmetics, Kolmar BNH needed to strengthen its food business and decided in 2012 to merge with Korea Foodipharm. Its CEO Jung Hwa-young was a founding member of Rexgene Biotech — the predecessor of Novarex — and had led sales for about ten years, making him a specialist in health functional foods; Korea Foodipharm had already recorded KRW 10.4 billion in revenue in 2011, supplying diet foods and multivitamins to LG Household & Health Care and Chong Kun Dang Bio. The merger strengthened OEM production capability and secured new customer channels, and Jung Hwa-young was appointed co-CEO alongside the incumbent, Kim Chi-bong.

Strengthening OEM/ODM capability. Initially specialised in productisation technology, Kolmar BNH achieved a return rate of 0.08% through a policy of contract farming for Cnidium officinale, Angelica gigas and white peony and quality inspection across every process step, and in 2012 completed a dedicated HEMOHIM plant in Sejong that strengthened automated processing. It went on to secure capability in bespoke formulations — colour multi-tip tablets, plant-based soft capsules, chewables, effervescents and troches — diversifying its customers to include E-mart and Yuhan. Through open-innovation-based joint research (C&D projects) it also built a pipeline of more than 20 new individually recognised ingredients. A leading example is unripe bitter melon ethanol extract powder, which obtained individual recognition in 2021 after six years of research with the Rural Development Administration and Gyeongsang National University; "Yuhan Blood Sugar Care Bitter Melon S52," launched through Yuhan in 2022, recorded about KRW 5 billion in sales.

5.3 Comparison with a competitor — Novarex

Novarex, a major competitor, entered the domestic health functional food OEM market as its beachhead with no ingredient development technology of its own, focusing on production capability, and later came to hold 40 individually recognised ingredients — the most in Korea — through its own ingredient development. It did not, however, build a pipeline with strong market impact, and its overseas expansion relied on contacting and contracting with individual customers, leaving exports at just 3% of revenue in 2021. Where Kolmar BNH achieved operating margins approaching 15% through high-volume production of a limited range, Novarex, producing a wide range of products, stayed at the industry-average 10–12% and fell behind on investment as well. In short, Novarex succeeded in entering its initial beachhead market but failed to judge properly, when identifying adjacent markets, whether its products could be applied there directly — a failure to develop a tailored strategy that cost it its position in the industry rankings.

Implications
  • Success factors seen through strategy: The literature on research institute companies has analysed success through the lens of capability — technological capability, entrepreneurial capability, founder commitment. This study suggests that market entry strategy and expansion strategy may matter more.
  • Do not confuse the beachhead market: Because research institute companies begin with a strong technology source, they often overestimate their technological capability and confuse the total market with the beachhead market. They should target a clearly segmented market and deliver their value proposition and message consistently.
  • Expand into adjacent markets: Building on the beachhead market secured, it is important to attempt expansion into connected adjacent markets.
  • Limitations: The study covers a single case, so further research on other research institute companies is needed.
