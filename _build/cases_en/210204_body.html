Keeping pace with the Fourth Industrial Revolution, Korea aims to strengthen the capabilities of small and medium-sized manufacturers through a government-led smart factory dissemination programme. Yet most adopting firms remain at a basic level and face a range of difficulties arising from the gap between existing work practices and the new systems. This study compares the smart factory build-out at POSCO — the only Korean company named to the World Economic Forum's "Lighthouse Factory" list — against the state of smart factory adoption in Korea, and proposes a direction for Korea's smart factory innovation strategy.

Research summary
  • Keeping pace with the Fourth Industrial Revolution, Korean manufacturing needs to secure competitiveness by building smart factories suited to the country's industrial structure. Manufacturing powers such as Germany, the United States, Japan and China are pursuing smart manufacturing innovation tailored to their own industrial conditions, and Korea seeks to strengthen manufacturing capability through a smart factory dissemination programme for SMEs.
  • Most SMEs that have adopted smart factories, however, remain at a basic level, and most carry a range of problems stemming from the gap between existing work practices and the systems.
  • This study compares the state of smart factory adoption in Korea with POSCO's own build-out, centring on POSCO as a case selected as a Lighthouse Factory leading global manufacturing through a successful smart factory implementation.
  • The comparison shows that Korea's smart factory programme focuses only on installing systems, whereas POSCO pursued process innovation and system adoption together, raising the fit between systems and shop-floor work.
  • On this basis, the study proposes a direction for Korea's smart factory innovation strategy: creating a verification function for start-up software, providing maintenance support, and inducing process innovation before systems are installed.

1. Introduction

According to UNIDO's Competitive Industrial Performance (CIP) Index, Korea ranks third among 152 countries in manufacturing competitiveness. Yet the number of products in which Korea holds the top global market share has stalled, the supply of skilled technicians has stagnated, and the hollowing out of manufacturing through offshoring is proceeding rapidly — making competitiveness through manufacturing innovation urgent. Major manufacturing powers are actively pursuing smart manufacturing strategies suited to their own industrial constraints: Germany through government-led public standardisation, the United States through market-based standards led by large firms, Japan through loosened standards complementing JIT and kaizen, and China through a government focus on priority industries. Korea likewise runs a government-led smart manufacturing innovation policy centred on SMEs. Against this backdrop, POSCO is the only Korean company selected for the World Economic Forum's "Lighthouse Factory" list, which the Forum has awarded since 2018, and is regarded as an exemplary case of smart factory implementation.

2. The state of smart factory adoption in Korea

Policy development and adoption by level

In June 2014 the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy announced its "Manufacturing Innovation 3.0" strategy and established the Smart Factory Promotion Team (now the Korea Smart Manufacturing Office) to make 10,000 SME factories smart by 2020. In 2018 the target was raised to 30,000, with goals of training 100,000 specialists, creating 66,000 jobs and generating KRW 18 trillion in additional revenue. In 2020 the government built KAMP (Korea AI Manufacturing Platform), a public–private manufacturing platform supporting advancement centred on data and AI. The Smart Manufacturing Office classifies adoption into basic, intermediate 1, intermediate 2 and advanced levels, and set an SME-tailored strategy centred on supplying five solutions: FA, MES, ERP, PLM and SCM. As of 2019, however, 8,941 of 11,488 adopting firms (77.8%) remained at the basic level, and data-centred smart transformation such as MES and ERP adoption was at a very low level.

SME perceptions and difficulties

The 2019 SME Informatisation Survey found that although 81.2% of SME CEOs and executives were highly aware of informatisation, only 12.5% of SMEs actually carried out process innovation for it. The contribution of information systems to improving work efficiency was also rated low, at about 30%. Firms that adopted smart factories reported five problems: ① defects and instability in the systems built; ② difficulty in improving and maintaining them; ③ the absence of the expected improvement in work; ④ difficulty applying them because they did not fit existing work systems; and ⑤ the complexity of additional work such as training and process improvement. Beyond system defects and maintenance difficulties, the absence of expected benefits and the additional work created by a poor fit with existing practice both point to a distance between ICT technology and the realities of the shop floor.

3. POSCO's smart factory build-out

Founded in 1968 as a state-owned enterprise, Pohang Iron and Steel (POSCO) was fully privatised in 2000 and has grown into a large steelmaker with revenue of KRW 57.79 trillion and operating profit of KRW 2.40 trillion as of 2020. It holds the top share of Korea's crude steel market and ranks fifth in the world by steel output, and it has held first place for several years in the WSD steel competitiveness assessment.

POSCO's Process Innovation (PI) programme, begun in 1999, sought to shift from producer-centred to customer-oriented thinking in preparation for privatisation, and was pursued in earnest to meet intensifying competition from the merger and consolidation of European and Japanese rivals and from the decision of its largest customer, Hyundai Motor Group, to operate its own blast furnace. POSCO achieved process innovation in two- to three-year cycles from PI phase 1 through phase 4, and on that foundation introduced suitable systems, successfully deploying the smart factory platform PosFrame in 2015.

From PI phase 1 to POSTIM

PI phase 1 (1999–2001) launched a dedicated PI office on the strength of chairman Yoo Sang-boo's drive and support, reviewing company-wide processes from a blank sheet and building customer-centred processes and management systems. With POSPIA, an integrated company-wide online management system, delivery time for hot-rolled products fell from 30 days to 14, dramatically improving business speed. PI phase 2 (2002–2005) introduced Six Sigma to change the way work was done and built MES and customer relationship management systems. By 2007, 35.6% of all employees and 80% of team leaders and above held a Six Sigma belt; across waves 1 to 8, a total of 7,126 projects generated KRW 730.8 billion in financial results. But the heavy and demanding Six Sigma methodology made participation by all employees difficult.

PI phase 3 (2006–2008) moved away from a traditional Six Sigma approach centred on a small elite and headline projects, developing QSS (Quick Six Sigma), which the operators who make up 70% of the shop floor could run easily and quickly. Combining this with BPM, POSCO built its own PSSM (POSCO Six Sigma Model) and embedded it through a culture of study circles. In 2008, when phase 3 concluded, revenue reached KRW 30.64 trillion and operating profit KRW 6.54 trillion, up 51.8% year on year. PI phase 4 (from 2009) and POSTIM (from 2014) pursued a shared-growth strategy extending innovation results and know-how to affiliates, suppliers and customers; through three activities — PSS+, QSS+ and SWP — they achieved a 22% reduction in quality non-conformance, a 32% reduction in equipment failures and a 39% reduction in lost-time injury frequency against 2013, along with the elimination of 3 million unnecessary documents.

From PosFrame to IXOTIVE

PosFrame is POSCO's proprietary smart factory platform, which collects, organises and stores the large volumes of data generated in steel production and applies advanced data analytics and artificial intelligence to build models for quality prediction and equipment failure forecasting. POSCO began development in July 2015 with the Gwangyang plate mill as a pilot, extended the project to the Pohang No. 2 hot strip mill in October of the same year, and launched a Smart Solution Council (SSC) to widen the scope across the company and the group. Through 321 smart projects run between 2016 and 2019, it achieved KRW 250 billion in cost savings. In 2021 it introduced IXOTIVE, a next-generation platform adding the PosMaster equipment control device and the voice-recognition equipment control system Boris to PosFrame, unveiling it for the first time at CES 2021.

4. Theoretical analysis of the POSCO case

Process innovation and system adoption through repeated testing and validation

POSCO's approach to innovation combines Rogers' divergent experimental method with Eric Ries' lean startup feedback loop. PI phase 1 built a structure suited to POSPIA 1.0 through customer-centred process redesign and company-wide reorganisation; phase 2 introduced Six Sigma; and phase 3 produced PSSM, reconfigured for the shop floor. From phase 4 onward, the POSTIM methodology has been further refined to define problems and improve them continuously.

A stable smart factory built through staged architectural innovation

POSCO's smart factory build-out did not follow a big-bang strategy of making every facility smart from the outset; it began on a small scale at the Gwangyang plate mill. That stage rested on stable evaluation in a modular form, after which the scope widened to the Pohang No. 2 hot strip mill to examine the complexity of connecting plants to one another. Through that examination the company developed the PosFrame platform, which links all plants organically, shifting to an integral form. In making the whole group smart, it then optimised the ecosystem value chain including suppliers and customers and modularised the external interfaces, developing an Integral Inside–Modular Outside (IIMO) form. Finally, working with DPR Construction, it built a cloud-based digital twin smart factory, further strengthening the internal integral architecture.

Making POSCO's entire production line smart is best understood not as an abrupt disruptive innovation strategy but as incremental architectural innovation achieved through testing and validation.

POSCO's shared-growth open innovation

POSCO has adopted an outside-in mode of open innovation, discovering promising firms through joint research with industry, academia and research institutes and through agreements with the Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the Korean Venture Capital Association. It also runs a "POSCO Venture Platform" built on venture valleys and venture funds to identify new growth businesses.

5. Comparing Korea's smart factory programme with the POSCO case

In system adoption, Korea's smart factory programme focuses only on installing systems rather than improving work processes, with a structure that adjusts shop-floor processes to fit them. POSCO, by contrast, had its practitioners improve work processes directly through a continuous feedback loop and pursued this in parallel with system adoption, raising the fit of the work. In architectural terms the stages on both sides are the same, but POSCO carried out an architectural transition driven by process innovation, whereas Korea's programme stops at an architectural transition with process innovation left out. That 98.3% of firms — 77.8% at the basic level and 20.5% at intermediate level 1 — fail to reach integration can be read as the result of an absence of process innovation, or of the systematic innovation POSCO practised. In the means of filtering technology, POSCO validates and filters new technologies through its own IT organisation, POSCO ICT, before applying them, whereas KAMP has no intermediary body to test and verify whether software supplied by start-ups runs reliably in practice.

6. A proposed direction for Korea's smart factory innovation strategy

This study identifies three problems with Korea's smart factory programme. First, systems and work are not continuously improved through a repeated feedback loop. Second, unlike POSCO, the programme focuses solely on architectural transition rather than accompanying it with process innovation. Third, the absence of an intermediary management function leaves no way to filter adopted systems and new technologies appropriately. Korea's smart factory programme currently reads as a fashionable innovation activity that simply imitates trends among manufacturing powers, and its completeness — centred on building infrastructure — is not high.

Implications
  • Create a verification function: System defects and instability are tied to the maturity of start-ups' systems, and can be improved by creating a unit within KAMP to test and verify start-up software.
  • Support maintenance: The difficulty of maintenance, which stems from SMEs having no IT department, can be offset by system maintenance support delivered through KAMP.
  • Pair adoption with process innovation: The remaining difficulties arise from system adoption without process innovation. Training in Six Sigma process innovation methods at the level of the Smart Manufacturing Office, together with inducing SMEs to carry out process innovation themselves before adopting systems, is needed.
  • Recognising the limits: There are differences between continuous-process and discrete industries, and between large firms and SMEs in scale. But the purpose of adopting new systems — to make work more efficient and raise corporate value and profit — is the same. These problems should be analysed independently of industry characteristics or firm size.
